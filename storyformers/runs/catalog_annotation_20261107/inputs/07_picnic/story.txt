At eleven o’clock, Bea and Sam carried Aunt June’s blue picnic blanket down to the riverside meadow. They had asked to borrow it, and Aunt June had handed it over with a warning.

“Bring it back without grass stains,” she said. “And no lemonade on the fringe.”

“We’ll be careful,” Bea promised.

They had packed sandwiches, pears, cheese, and a jug of lemonade for their cousin Theo, who had moved to town last month. He was supposed to meet them at noon. Bea had made a welcome sign in green crayon. Sam had packed an extra cookie because, he said, “A welcome needs a cookie.”

They chose a flat spot well above the riverbank. Bea spread the blanket and smoothed every wrinkle. Sam set the jug in the middle, where it could not tip, then arranged three plates.

“Your sign is upside down,” he said.

“It is not.”

“The words are facing the blanket.”

Bea turned it around and pinned it to the basket with a clothespin. “There. Now Theo will know we’re happy he came.”

Sam arranged the pear slices in a sunburst. Bea set the biggest sandwich on Theo’s plate.

“He might like cheese better,” Sam said.

“He told Aunt June he likes cheese.”

“I like cheese.”

“It’s Theo’s lunch.”

Sam sighed. “I know. I’m hungry, too.”

They heard a bicycle bell on the path, but it belonged to a man with a dog. At twelve-thirty, they heard another bell, far away. No Theo. The sun crept over the grass, and the lemonade jug grew warm.

“Maybe he got lost,” Bea said.

“Or maybe he’s not coming,” Sam said, though he kept watching the path.

Just then a gust rushed across the meadow. The welcome sign tore free, the napkins flew, and one corner of the blanket lifted like a sail. The basket began to slide toward the river.

“Grab it!” Bea shouted.

Sam caught the basket handle. Bea planted both hands on the blanket’s edge. The sign skittered across the grass, past a patch of buttercups, and snagged in the reeds near the water.

Bea saw it go. She could run for the sign, but the blanket was pulling hard against her fingers. The jug wobbled inside the basket.

“Let it go!” Sam called.

Bea leaned her weight onto the blanket. Sam dragged the basket back with a grunt. The sign stayed in the reeds, its green letters flapping. One sandwich tumbled into the grass, and the pear sunburst scattered across the blanket.

For a moment, neither cousin spoke.

“We could pack up,” Sam said. “The food’s all mixed up.”

Bea looked at the empty place setting. “Theo might still come.”

She picked up the cleanest sandwich and wrapped it in a napkin. “Let’s save this one for him.”

Sam glanced at the cookie in his pocket, then at the sandwich. His stomach gave a small, loud growl.

“Fine,” he said. “But I’m saving the cookie for me.”

They weighted the blanket’s corners with smooth stones and gathered what food they could. Sam wiped the jug dry with a napkin. Bea brushed grass from the cheese. They sat together and waited, listening to the river slip over its stones.

At last, a boy appeared on the path, pushing a bicycle with a flat tire. His hair stuck up on one side, and one trouser cuff was muddy.

“I’m sorry!” he called. “The bridge path was closed, and then my tire went flat.”

“Theo!” Bea stood up so quickly she nearly kicked over the jug.

Theo looked at the scattered lunch. “Did the wind do all that?”

“It also stole our sign,” Sam said. “It’s in the reeds.”

Theo peered toward the river, then turned back. “I’m glad you didn’t go after it. I’d have missed you.”

Bea held out the wrapped sandwich. “We saved this for you.”

Theo broke it neatly in three. “Then we can all have some.” Sam handed over the cookie, which Theo split too.

They ate their crooked little lunch, trading pear slices and stories about the new neighborhood. When the plates were empty, Theo helped them shake the crumbs from Aunt June’s blanket. The blue checks billowed once in the breeze, then settled across their arms. They folded it together, with the green-crayon sign—still readable, though bent—tucked safely inside the blanket’s fringe.
