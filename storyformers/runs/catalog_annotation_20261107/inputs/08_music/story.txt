Every Thursday, the community music room filled with sounds that didn’t quite agree: a piano practicing scales, a flute making long, sleepy notes, and someone in the corner trying to teach a tambourine to behave.

Nia liked the little drum best. It was just the right size to tuck beneath her arm, with a red ribbon knotted around its handle. Ms. Dalia let her borrow it for the week.

“I want to play a duet at Saturday’s music-room picnic,” Nia told her. “Something with a drumbeat and an answer.”

“Then you’ll need someone to answer,” said Ms. Dalia.

That afternoon, a new boy came in holding a music folder against his chest. His name was Mateo. He sat near the door and watched the other children play.

Nia tested a beat on her drum: bum-bum, pause, bum.

Mateo’s fingers tapped the same rhythm on his knees.

Nia noticed, but she didn’t point. She played the beat again, softer this time. Mateo’s fingers answered.

When the song ended, she asked, “Would you like to make a duet with me for Saturday?”

Mateo looked toward the roomful of children. “I like playing. I just don’t want everybody looking at me yet.”

“Okay,” Nia said.

She didn’t ask again. She carried her drum to the rug and practiced there, leaving little pauses in her beat. The red ribbon bobbed whenever she played.

Mateo stayed by the door. Sometimes his fingers tapped. Sometimes they didn’t.

On Friday, Ms. Dalia set a signup sheet beside the piano. “I need the names for Saturday’s program before we lock up,” she said. “There’s room for one more act.”

Nia had imagined Mateo beside her, answering her drum with a second drum from the shelf. She looked at him. He was studying the signup sheet, his mouth pressed into a thin line.

She could write both their names. Mateo might say yes once he saw them there. But that would make the choice for him.

Nia took the pencil and wrote, “Nia—drum solo.”

It felt like putting away a favorite toy before she was finished playing.

Mateo glanced at the page. “You could still do a duet,” he said quietly.

“I could,” Nia replied. “But I don’t know if you want one.”

He looked down at his folder. “Not on the program.”

“Then it’s a solo.”

Nia practiced her song that evening in the kitchen. Without another player, the pauses seemed enormous. Her father offered to clap along, but she shook her head. She tried making the beats softer and louder, like footsteps coming down a hall. The song began to sound less empty.

On Saturday, the community music room was crowded with folding chairs and paper cups of lemonade. Nia’s drum waited beside her stool. The ribbon lay against its wooden rim.

She could see Mateo in the back row, still holding his folder.

Ms. Dalia introduced the next act. “Nia, with a drum solo.”

Nia sat down. Her palms felt warm. She played bum-bum, pause, bum. The pause opened wide. She filled it with a quieter beat, then another, making a path through the song.

From the back row came a faint tap.

Nia kept her eyes on the drum.

Tap-tap.

She played her next pattern. The answering taps grew clearer, though Mateo hadn’t left his chair. Nia’s shoulders loosened. She finished the song with three soft beats and let the last one ring.

The room clapped.

Mateo stood, then stopped. He looked at the second small drum on the instrument shelf. It was the same size as Nia’s.

Nia didn’t wave him over. She simply set her drum beside her stool and waited while Ms. Dalia thanked the audience.

Mateo crossed to the shelf and lifted the second drum. “Could I try the answer part?” he asked.

“Sure,” said Nia.

They sat on the rug, not the little performance space. Mateo tapped once, testing the sound. Nia answered. He tried two beats. She left him room for three.

By the time the lemonade was gone, their duet had no name, no program listing, and no ending they had agreed on. They kept playing anyway.

When it was time to put the instruments away, Nia returned her borrowed drum to the shelf. Mateo placed the other beside it. The red ribbon hung between the two handles, quivering faintly as the last sound faded.
