At three o’clock, the local museum’s activity room smelled of paste, old books, and the orange peels in Kit’s pocket. Miri had arranged six chairs in a neat row for the shadow exhibit. She wanted Mr. Bell to choose her as a junior guide for Saturday. Kit wanted his little sister, Dot, to stop calling museums “rooms where you can’t touch anything.”

“Today,” Miri said, holding up the label, “we’ll prove that moving the moth toward the screen makes its shadow smaller.”

“And then,” Kit said, lifting his cardboard dragon, “we’ll make one big enough to eat the moon.”

“The dragon is not on the label.”

“It’s an improved moth.”

They borrowed a torch from Mr. Bell at the front desk. He handed it to Miri, who promised to bring it back before closing. In the activity room, they stretched a white sheet between two chairs and set the moth on the wooden track in front of it. The label showed a torch at one end, a screen at the other, and a little arrow pointing the moth toward the screen.

Miri held the torch. Kit slid the moth along the track.

The shadow spread wider across the sheet.

Miri stopped him. “That’s the opposite.”

“Maybe the label’s wrong,” said Kit. He sounded pleased. A wrong label might be more exciting than a correct one.

They tried again. Miri leaned forward to keep the beam on the moth as Kit moved it. This time the shadow grew until its wings nearly touched both chairs.

“See?” Kit said. “Dragon-sized.”

“That doesn’t prove anything,” Miri said, though she was looking at the label as if it had insulted her.

Kit moved the moth back. Miri moved the torch too, keeping the bright circle centered. The shadow jumped and wobbled.

“Hold still,” Miri told herself. She planted her feet. Her arms began to ache. When Kit slid the moth forward, the beam slipped off its paper wings.

From the corridor came the rumble of arriving voices. Dot’s laugh floated above them.

Miri glanced at the chairs. “They’re early.”

Kit peeked through the doorway. “Dot’s with them. I told her there’d be a dragon.”

Miri’s stomach tightened. Mr. Bell might be among the visitors. If she started with a shadow that contradicted the label, he might never ask her to guide anyone.

Kit pointed at the small print beneath the diagram. “There. ‘Keep the torch on the star.’”

A yellow star was printed at the left end of the track. Miri had been so busy making the beam follow the moth that she had never noticed it.

“We could say the torch is faulty,” Kit whispered. “Then just do the dragon.”

Miri looked at the visitors gathering outside. She could start her speech now and hope nobody asked. Instead, she put the torch down on two books, wedged its handle with a lump of modeling clay, and turned the beam toward the sheet.

“Wait,” she called. “Our first try didn’t match the label. We moved the torch, too. Can you give us one minute?”

A boy in a red scarf stepped into the room. Dot peered around his elbow.

Miri slid the moth back to the start. Kit watched the torch, ready to catch it if it shifted. Miri pushed the moth toward the screen. Its shadow shrank from the size of a dinner plate to the size of a saucer.

“Oh,” said Dot.

“The torch stayed put,” Miri said. “When the moth was nearer the torch, its shadow spread out. Near the screen, there’s less room for it to spread before it lands.”

Kit lifted his dragon. “And this is what happens when you put something close to the torch.”

He held the dragon near the beam. Its jagged shadow filled the sheet, tail curling over one chair and wings climbing almost to the top.

Dot squealed. “It’s eating the moon!”

“It’s an improved moth,” Kit said, grinning.

Miri handed the dragon to Dot. “You can try. Just leave the torch on the star.”

Dot held the cutout near the screen. Its shadow became a small, tidy shape. Then she raised two fingers beside it, and their shadows fluttered together on the white sheet: one paper moth, one little pair of living wings.
