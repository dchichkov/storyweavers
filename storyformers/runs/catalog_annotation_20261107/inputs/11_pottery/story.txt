Every Thursday, Bea walked past the bakery and the bus stop to Mr. Ivo’s pottery workshop. For three weeks, she had been making a cup for Grandpa Len. His fingers grew stiff in the mornings, and the thin handle on his old mug was hard for him to hold.

“I want the handle wide enough for two fingers,” Bea told Mr. Ivo. “And the cup needs to stand steady. Grandpa carries his tea out to the garden.”

“Good things to think about,” said Mr. Ivo. He set a lump of clay on her wheel. “What else?”

Bea pictured Grandpa’s hands around his mug. “Something for his thumb, too.”

She centered the clay and pulled it upward into a round cup. Then she borrowed Mr. Ivo’s wooden shaping rib—a smooth, curved tool—and pressed it gently against the outside wall.

The rib slipped.

A deep dent appeared in the clay. The cup’s side bulged beside it, and the rim went a little crooked.

Bea stopped turning the wheel. “Oh no.”

She dabbed the dent with a damp sponge, hoping to smooth it away. Instead, the wall shifted farther inward.

Mr. Ivo leaned over to look. “It’s still thick enough,” he said. “But if you keep pushing, you might make a hole.”

“Can I fill the dent with more clay?”

“We could try,” he said. “But a patch might not stick well. You could also slice the whole cup apart and start again.”

Bea glanced at the clock. The workshop’s next kiln firing was the last one before Grandpa’s birthday. If she started over, there wouldn’t be time for the new cup to dry and be fired. She could give him a store-bought mug instead. Or she could keep the crooked one, even though she had wanted it to be just right.

She set the cup carefully on the table. It didn’t wobble. The rim leaned a little, but not enough to spill from.

Bea slipped two fingers through the wide handle and rested her thumb against the dent.

Her hand settled comfortably.

“What if I leave this part?” she asked. “It could be a place for his thumb.”

Mr. Ivo nodded. “You can make that choice. Just smooth the edges so it feels good, and check that the inside wall is thick all the way around.”

Bea ran a finger along the inside. Then, with the soft side of the sponge, she eased the sharp edges of the dent. She turned the handle so it faced the hollow, opposite the thumb rest. The cup still looked crooked, but now the crookedness seemed to point toward a hand.

When she finished, Mr. Ivo held out his hand for the shaping rib.

“Thank you for lending it,” Bea said, wiping the clay from its handle before returning it.

The cup dried on a shelf, then went into the kiln with the other pots. When Bea came back, her cup was pale and hard. She dipped it into a blue glaze, turning it slowly until every side was coated. The blue gathered in the thumb hollow like a little pool.

After the second firing, the cup stood on the pickup table with its handle tilted slightly to one side. Bea carried it home in a box padded with newspaper.

On Grandpa’s birthday, he opened the box at the kitchen table.

“It’s a cup,” Bea said quickly. “The side got dented, and then I made it into a thumb place. The handle is wide for your fingers.”

Grandpa turned the cup in his hands. He slipped two fingers through the handle and set his thumb in the hollow. His knuckles rested against the smooth clay.

“Well,” he said, “that’s just where my thumb wanted to go.”

He poured in water first and set the cup on the table. It stayed steady. Then he took it outside, filled with tea, and carried it to his garden bench.

The next morning, Bea found the cup on the bench beside Grandpa’s trowel. A ring of damp earth marked the table under it. Grandpa was kneeling among the bean plants, his thumb resting in the blue hollow as he drank. Beside his knee, the sun caught a tiny bead of water on the cup’s crooked rim.
