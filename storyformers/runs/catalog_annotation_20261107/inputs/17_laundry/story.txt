At four o’clock, Tessa saw the clouds gathering over the shared garden. They were dark underneath, like the bruised plums in Mrs. Bell’s tree.

Mrs. Bell stood beside the washing line, trying to lift a basket with one hand. Her other wrist was wrapped in a green brace.

“I can manage the shirts,” she said, “but that sheet is going to win.”

“I’ll hang it,” said Tessa. She wanted Mrs. Bell to trust her with an important job. Mrs. Bell had once let her water the seedlings in the greenhouse, and Tessa had been careful not to spill a drop.

“Would you? I need to catch the pharmacy before it closes.” Mrs. Bell handed her a yellow peg bag. “Borrow this. The blue pegs grip best. Use two on each corner of the sheet.”

Tessa hooked the bag over her shoulder. At the far end of the line, Mr. Osei’s tea towels flapped in the breeze. She left them plenty of room.

First came the socks. Tessa paired them, toe to toe, and clipped them neatly. Then she hung Mrs. Bell’s shirts by their hems, so the wind wouldn’t stretch the shoulders. A gust rattled the shed door.

The sheet lay folded in the basket, white with a blue border. Tessa shook it open. It billowed at once, ballooning above her head. For a moment she couldn’t see anything but cloth.

“Down,” she told it.

She caught one corner, then another. The line dipped in the middle. Its wooden prop—the forked stick that held it high—skidded across the grass.

Tessa looked at the blue pegs in her bag. She could pin the sheet fast and finish quickly. But the line was already sagging, and Mr. Osei’s tea towels were close by. If she pulled the sheet tight, it might drag everything down.

The easy thing, she thought, was to pretend she hadn’t noticed the prop slipping. Mrs. Bell would be back soon. But Tessa pictured the clean shirts tumbling into the muddy grass.

“Mr. Osei!” she called.

He appeared from behind the greenhouse with a trowel in his hand. “What’s the matter?”

“The prop’s sliding. Could you hold the sheet while I move the clothes?”

He put down his trowel and took the billowing corner. Tessa unclipped the shirts and socks she had hung. She laid them carefully in the empty basket, keeping them off the ground.

“You can use the rain shelter,” Mr. Osei said. “There’s a line under the roof.”

Tessa had seen the short lines inside the wooden garden shelter, but she’d hoped she wouldn’t need them. Using them meant starting over.

“May I?” she asked.

“Of course. It’s for everyone.”

They carried the basket beneath the shelter. Mr. Osei held the sheet while Tessa folded it lengthwise, then draped it over the sheltered line. The white cloth sagged in the middle.

“More pegs,” Tessa said.

She used two blue pegs at each corner, just as Mrs. Bell had told her. Then she worked along the edges, clipping the sheet securely. Mr. Osei passed her shirts and socks from the basket, and she filled the rest of the line.

The first raindrops struck the path. Tessa’s hair prickled with cold. She reached for the last little sock, but a gust shoved it from her hand. It sailed toward the shelter entrance.

Mr. Osei caught it against his trousers.

“Saved,” he said, handing it back.

Tessa pegged the sock between its mate and a striped shirt. Outside, rain pattered on the shed roof and darkened the garden beds. Inside the shelter, the washing stirred gently in the warm air.

Mrs. Bell came through the gate carrying a small paper bag from the pharmacy. Her eyes went to the empty basket, then to the sheet hanging beneath the roof.

“I thought you’d have everything on the big line,” she said.

“The prop slid,” Tessa replied. She lifted the yellow peg bag off her shoulder. “So we moved it.”

Mrs. Bell took the bag and squeezed Tessa’s shoulder with her good hand. “Good thinking. And look—nothing in the mud.”

Tessa glanced at the white sheet. The blue border hung straight, held by bright blue pegs. Beyond the shelter, rain stitched silver lines across the garden path. Inside, one tiny sock swung beside its pair.
