Snow had softened the edges of the village square. It rounded the fountain, buried the benches, and laid white caps on every roof. Nia stood beside Mr. Vale’s shop with a basket of groceries on a red sled. The basket held a loaf, carrots, tea, and a bundle of kindling.

“Mrs. Wren asked for these before the snow gets deeper,” Mr. Vale said, tying the basket down. “And she asked for you.”

Nia tugged her mittens snug. “I promised I’d go.”

Across the square, Tomas was polishing the runners of his blue sled with a rag. He had spent all week boasting that it was the fastest sled in the village. Now he planted one boot on its back and grinned at Nia.

“Race me to the old mill post and back,” he called. “I want to see if your red sled can keep up.”

Nia looked at the basket. Then she looked at the hill. She loved racing Tomas. She had beaten him once, by half a sled, and he had spent three days insisting the snow had slowed him down.

“After I deliver these,” she said. “Mrs. Wren’s fire is nearly out.”

Tomas glanced at the sky. “The race will be over by then.”

“I know.”

He gave his runners one last wipe. “All right. But I’m going now.”

The village bell rang, and children pushed off from the square. Tomas sprang onto his sled and shot down the main lane, his blue scarf streaming behind him. Nia watched him vanish around the bend, then leaned into the red sled’s rope.

Mrs. Wren lived up a narrow side lane. Snow had drifted against the fences, and Nia had to pull hard. At the turn, the red sled’s runner caught beneath a ridge of ice. The sled jolted sideways. The basket tipped, and the loaf slid halfway out.

Nia caught it with both hands. Her mittened fingers slipped on the rough basket rim. She set the loaf back, tightened the rope, and tried to haul the sled free. It wouldn’t budge.

From farther down the lane came the hiss of runners over snow. Tomas swept around the bend, close to the end of his race. The square lay just beyond him, and he was in the lead.

“Tomas!” Nia called. “Could you help me?”

He looked from her jammed sled to the square. The other racers were nearly there. For a moment he kept his hands on his rope.

Then he planted his boots. His blue sled slid past him and bumped softly into a snowbank.

“Hold the basket,” he said, hurrying over. “I’ll lift the back.”

Together they rocked the red sled loose. Tomas pressed his shoulder against it while Nia pulled. The runner popped free, and the sled lurched forward. One carrot rolled out of the basket. Tomas caught it before it disappeared under the snow.

“You’ve lost your race,” Nia said.

Tomas tucked the carrot back beside the loaf. “I know.”

At Mrs. Wren’s gate, they found another drift piled high against the latch. Nia dug with her boots while Tomas used the flat of his mittened hands. They cleared a narrow gap, then dragged the sled through, taking care not to tip the basket again.

Mrs. Wren opened her door before they knocked. A gray shawl was wrapped around her shoulders, and a kettle sat cold on the table behind her.

“I was beginning to think the road had swallowed you,” she said.

“The sled got stuck,” Nia replied. “Tomas helped.”

Mrs. Wren’s eyes moved to the blue sled resting beside the gate. “And the race?”

Tomas brushed snow from his sleeves. “It went on without me.”

Mrs. Wren didn’t ask anything more. She carried the kindling to her stove while Nia set the groceries on the table. Soon a small flame caught. The kettle began to murmur, and the warm smell of carrots and onions rose from the pot.

Mrs. Wren poured tea into three cups. Tomas drank his standing up, glancing toward the window whenever a gust rattled it.

When they were ready to leave, she wrapped the last piece of bread in a cloth and handed it to Nia. “For the walk back. You two have done enough pulling today.”

They returned to the square as the afternoon light turned pale. Mr. Vale was stacking sleds by his shop. The children who had raced were stamping snow from their boots, and Tomas’s blue scarf was no longer streaming anywhere.

“Did you win?” Nia asked.

“I didn’t finish,” he said. He looked at the red sled. “But I still want to race you.”

Nia handed the red sled to Mr. Vale. “Could we borrow it for one lap around the square?”

Mr. Vale glanced at the tidy, empty basket, then nodded. “One lap. Bring it back before dark.”

Tomas and Nia lined up beside the fountain. At the bell’s next ring, they pushed off together. Their sleds skimmed over the fresh snow, runners drawing two bright lines around the square. Up the hill, Mrs. Wren’s window glowed with a steady golden light.
