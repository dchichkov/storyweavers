# Blind Prose Comparisons

Read each pair without opening provenance.json or model results.
Choose left, right or tie. Both stories may be weak. Different lengths and genres are intentional
but are confounds: do not infer population superiority from this small diagnostic set.

## p00

### Left

Ari thought the crate held stone slabs and wanted it on the handcart. "I can manage," Ari said, before anyone had suggested otherwise. The crate waited on the workshop floor. It had not agreed to anything.

The crate came up in Ari's hands and went onto the cart. That had been surprisingly easy. Ari looked at the lid. A success could be suspicious too.

Cy checked and found an empty wooden bottom. The contents and the first attempt now belonged to the same explanation.

Cy explained the result to Ari. Ari listened instead of repeating the first plan. "All right," Ari said. "Tell me what we actually have."

Ari took the empty crate off the cart and put it on the workbench. The impressive lift had delivered no stones. "I can manage this," Ari said, and this time meant the useful empty box.

The cart's space was empty again; the empty crate had a more honest job on the bench.

### Right

Once upon a time, there was a sleepy bright bear named Nibbles. Nibbles spent many days in the kitchen. It heard a small sound coming from a wooden case.
Nibbles opened it gently and discovered a sleepy puppy inside. The little animal looked hungry and scared.
Nibbles brought food and spoke in a soft voice. Slowly, the animal came closer.
By the end of the day, the discovery had become a new friend.
Nibbles learned that a discovery is better when it is shared.

## p01

### Left

The picnic boat needed cups. Ari had a box that was supposed to hold them, and a very short plan: put the box on the boat. Looking inside was not yet part of it.

Cy had an earlier arrangement: bring Bo the brass key at the jetty by the bell. Bo had already gone there. The key was still with Cy, and so was the promise.

Ari put the box aboard. "Done." The lid was still shut. Ari looked at it again. The boat now had a box; whether it had cups was a different question.

Ari tried to check more closely. Opening the lid revealed another wrapping. Nothing in view established that the boat had received cups.

Ari caught a free corner of the wrapping. "This part is not tied," Ari said, and lifted it away onto the bench. With it out of the way, Ari could see paint tins where Ari had expected cups.

"Could you take that box off the boat and put it by the sign?" Ari asked.
"Taking it off is part of the job now?" Cy said.
"I made it part of the job." Ari looked at the paint box. "It is not cups."

"Not before I take Bo the key," Cy said. "I promised."
Ari looked at the unfinished work. It was tempting to make it sound more important. That would not make the earlier promise disappear.

Ari took the paint box off the boat and carried it to the workbench. The second trip was as real as the first. "I can undo my part," Ari said. The box waited beside the unfinished sign instead of taking up room for cups.

Cy handed the brass key to Bo at the jetty by the promised time. The earlier arrangement had survived the new work, whether that work had been finished, shared or left for later.

The paint belonged beside the sign. Finding cups remained a separate job. Bo also had the key on time.

### Right

Once upon a time, there was a patient creative little boy named Sam. Sam spent the day in the playroom. There was also a creative kind little boy named Remy.
Sam and Remy were pretending to hunt for treasure. They reached for a blue vase, but it slipped and broke.
Sam felt scared, and Remy looked sorry. Mom came over and helped them clean the pieces safely.
Then Mom gave them a soft practice blanket to use gently. The new gift was a soft practice blanket.
Sam said, "Thank you, Mom." Remy said thank you too, and they kept the new gift on the table where it was safe.
Sam learned that saying thank you is part of taking care of a gift.

### Right

The dragon came to Rose's window at half past four.

"I require a thief," the dragon said.

Rose put down her book.

"How good a thief?" Rose asked.

"Better than the one who has robbed me."

"What did they take?"

"My emerald."

"How big?"

"Pea-sized."

Rose looked at him.

"I am allowed to like small things," the dragon said.

The culprit was a magpie. She had installed the jewel in a nest at the top of a narrow pine tree. The dragon could reach it easily. Unfortunately, reaching it would demolish the tree.

"I offered her a ruby," the dragon said.

"What happened?"

"She flew away."

"Did you roar?"

"I explained."

"Loudly?"

"There was some emphasis."

Rose sat beneath the tree and watched. The magpie returned with a twig. Each time she fitted it into the rim, it sprang loose. She pushed the jewel against it, but the twig slipped again.

"Your ruby. Show me," Rose said.

He opened his claw. It was the size of a cabbage.

"Well, she can't carry that," Rose said.

"It is worth a kingdom."

"She hasn't got a kingdom-sized pocket."

The dragon considered the ruby.

"I may have approached this as a dragon," the dragon said.

"She needs something to hold that twig."

"I could hold it."

"Until next spring?"

"I do have other appointments."

Rose borrowed a little tin dish from home. She put a bottle cap in it, a piece of blue glass, and a little loop of soft wire. She set it beneath the tree.

Then they moved away.

"Further," Rose said.

"I can scarcely see my emerald."

"She can still see your teeth."

He shut his mouth and took another careful step back.

"That helps," Rose said.

The magpie came down. She inspected the glass. She tipped the bottle cap over. She gripped the wire loop and tugged. Then she took it.

"Well, we've paid her," the dragon whispered.

"Wait."

The bird returned to the nest.

She hooked the loop around the troublesome twig. This time the twig stayed. She nudged away the jewel she had been using to prop it up. Then something green dropped through the branches.

The dragon caught it before it touched the grass. He stared at the emerald resting on his enormous claw.

"She threw it away," the dragon said.

"She found something she liked better."

"But..."

"Yes. A bent bit of wire."

They left the magpie settling her nest and took the path to Rose's house.

"What do I owe you?" the dragon asked.

Rose thought.

"A story. A true one. About somewhere you've been," Rose said.

"I know some very long stories."

"Good."

"And I occasionally make myself look rather magnificent."

"I'll ask questions."

The dragon smiled.

"Yes. I rather thought you would," the dragon said.

The emerald rested safely in his closed claw. Beside him, Rose was already thinking of her first question.
