Nell hid a paper crown under the village bakery counter. Tomorrow Mr Vale was retiring to the sea; tonight the village would surprise him with supper. Their plaited loaf waited in its tin. The windowless oven-room bulb had failed; the baker was fetching another from town. "His lantern," Kit suggested. "Ask without telling," Nell said. "Seven o'clock. Not a word."

They crossed the lane to the workshop. "Could we borrow your lantern? Our bulb's gone," Nell asked. Vale lifted it off its hook. "Bring it back before I lock up. I need it for the cellar steps. Leave oil for them." Nell took the handle. Back across the lane, Kit held the bakery door for her and the borrowed light.

Nell checked the oil, then carried the lantern into the oven room. The wheel stuck when she tried it. She eased the bent guard aside; it turned. She lit it and set it on the stone shelf, clear of the towels. Kit helped slide the loaf into the oven. "Everybody's bread," he said. Nell closed the door gently. For ten minutes the plaits swelled in the heat.

A knock reached them from the bakery. Nell extinguished the lantern and carried it out to answer. "Nearly locking-up time. May I have my lantern?" Vale asked. The loaf needed ten more minutes. Kit looked at Nell, then at the crown beneath the counter. She kept her hand around the borrowed handle.

"Could we keep it a little longer?" Nell asked. "Then I'll bring it straight over." Vale checked his watch. "Ten. I can pack the counter first." He went back across the lane, leaving the lantern in her hand. Kit let out his breath. Nell held up both hands, counting the minutes on her fingers.

Only Vale's cellar oil remained. Nell went in and switched the oven off. Back at the counter she left the baker a note: Can this loaf be saved? Then she wrote Vale another: Breakfast before you leave tomorrow, if we can manage it? Kit read both. "Don't promise bread yet." She underlined the question mark.

They delivered the invitation across the lane, then returned. Vale checked the returned lantern: wick free, oil for the steps. Breakfast was only a question in his pocket. Kit set two cups on the counter. Nell laid the crown between them. The loaf was still unfinished; the unanswered note for the baker lay beside the empty places.

## greenhouse: Seed 1000, 7 Scenes

Sun glittered on the allotment greenhouse. Mara arrived with a waxed paper parcel of replacement beans. She had forgotten Ivo's watering, then blamed the tap. She wanted her friend back. At the gate she shifted the stone jamming its foot, pulled it open and carried the parcel up the path.

"Can I come in?" Mara asked. Ivo lifted his bucket away from the doorway. "Mind the latch." She opened the door, carried the parcel inside and put it on the dry bench before shutting the door. The dead bean stalks stood in their row. He had not thrown them away yet.

"The beans are for you," Mara said. Ivo left the string alone. "Did the shop have a tap too?" Her prepared excuses deserted her. "I forgot. The tap worked. Then I lied." He pressed a thumb into the bench soil. "I spent an hour taking it apart." This time she did not explain herself away.

The greenhouse had grown close and breathless. Ivo's young tomatoes drooped towards the hot bench. He pulled the vent cord, but the cord parted and the pane stayed shut. Mara watched a leaf curl against the glass. "It has to stay open," Ivo said. "Not just while someone stands here holding it."

The tray sagged in Mara's hands. "Tape the wooden stay?" Ivo asked. "Moving them will be quicker," Mara said. She sheltered the parcel and opened the door. Ivo joined her. They spent half an hour freeing rooted pots for the shaded lower bench. Leaves wilted first; by the last trip, two seedlings had scorched leaves. They were alive, but those leaves would not recover. The tape lay unused.

"Not another tray today," Ivo said, looking at the damaged seedlings. She asked for his sandwich tin; Ivo opened it. She unwrapped the beans and spread them inside. She set the open tin on the shelf. "Can I come back to help?" Mara asked. "Saturday. While I am here," Ivo said. She nodded, leaving the beans unsown.

They stepped onto the path and Mara closed the door. Ivo kept the key. "Knock on Saturday." She nodded without asking for it. Through the glass stood an open tin of unsown beans. There would be no coming and going on her own yet. She waited until he had finished speaking before turning towards the gate.

Wind rattled the allotment greenhouse. Mara arrived with a waxed paper parcel of replacement beans. She had forgotten Ivo's watering, then blamed the tap. She wanted her friend back. At the gate she shifted the stone jamming its foot, pulled it open and carried the parcel up the path.

Mara pushed the parcel towards him. "The tap must have stuck. These should replace them." Ivo pushed it back. "I checked the tap. There was nothing wrong with it." She kept her hands beside the parcel instead of answering. Between them the string lay in a neat bow, still untied.

A gust lifted the roof vent. Rain blew through the gap onto Ivo's young tomatoes, bending the matchstick stems. He pulled the closing cord; it came away in his hand. Mara caught the swinging end. Neither pull had moved the vent. "We need to hold it down," he said, looking up at the next cloud.

Mara tested the roll. Enough tape remained. Ivo lowered the frame while she wedged the notched wooden stay between its handle and the bracket. She taped the stay's foot to the bracket. They let go; rain stopped reaching the tray. The parcel stayed tied on the bench.

Ivo gathered the beans from the parcel she opened and tipped them into Mara's cupped hands. "They are yours. I did not ask for replacements." She looked at the small hard shapes. "I helped, though." He nodded towards the vent. "That was help. It wasn't an answer about the tap."

Mara stepped outside, shut the door and walked back to the open gate with the beans in her hands. The empty waxed paper stayed on his bench. Ivo stayed by the greenhouse. She had brought something to leave behind and was carrying the important part away. At the gate she put the beans in her pocket, one careful handful at a time.
