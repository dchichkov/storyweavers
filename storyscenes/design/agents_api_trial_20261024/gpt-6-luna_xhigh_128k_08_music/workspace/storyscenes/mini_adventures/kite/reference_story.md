# The Beat Between Us

On Saturday afternoons, the community music room belonged to whoever arrived first. Mara arrived early because she had promised Ms Vale she would return the little hand drum before the children’s circle ended. She also hoped to play a duet at the open-room gathering. Her best friend was away, and she did not want her careful rhythm to sound like a solo again.

Ms Vale checked the drum’s wooden rim before handing it over. “It’s yours for the afternoon, not forever,” she said. Mara nodded solemnly. The drumhead was a little slack at one edge, but it still gave a warm, low sound. Ms Vale showed her how to tap near the middle without striking hard. Mara tried a simple pattern: two soft beats, then a pause.

“Could I invite someone new to answer it?” Mara asked. She had noticed June at the last music-room open day, sitting by the door while other children tried instruments. June had come back today, which Mara took as a hopeful sign. She imagined the two of them playing the pattern together, perhaps even showing it at the gathering.

The room was not quiet. A trumpet group practiced behind the folding partition, and someone in the hallway rolled a cart of music stands over a rough patch in the floor. Their sounds rattled together. Mara carried the drum to the circle of chairs. Most seats were already arranged, but one stool leaned against the wall. She dragged it closer and left a broad gap beside it.

June appeared in the doorway with her jacket still zipped. “Hi,” Mara said, keeping her feet where they were. “I’m Mara. This is the music room.”

“I know,” June answered. Her voice was low. “I’ve been here once.” She looked past the drum at the other players, then at the nearest chair. Her hands curled into her sleeves. Mara wanted to ask if she would play, but the question suddenly seemed larger than it had in her head.

Mara took the blue ribbon she had used to mark her place in a songbook. She tied one end loosely around the drum handle and let the rest hang where June could see it. “Sometimes I watch this when I’m learning a beat,” she said. “It moves a little when I tap.” She didn’t tell June what the ribbon meant or ask her to copy it.

Mara gave the drum a single quiet tap. The ribbon trembled. June glanced at the drum, then at the trumpet group. “I can’t tell which sound I’m supposed to follow,” she said. She didn’t sound annoyed, only unsure. Mara could have repeated the beat louder, but she remembered the room was already full of loud sounds.

Instead, Mara played the pattern once at a softer pace and rested her hands in her lap. “It’s not a test,” she said. “I’m still figuring it out.” She showed June the open chair and told her she could tap, listen, or pass. She meant the last option, though saying it made her worry that June would choose it.

June sat nearest the door. The chair legs squeaked, and she stopped moving. Mara looked down at the ribbon rather than watching June for an answer. The trumpet group stumbled through a run, stopped, and began again. For a while the only thing between them was the room’s busy noise and the quiet drum waiting on Mara’s knees.

Mara had planned to ask June to answer the rhythm. She had practiced the invitation in her head, with a bright smile and a joke about being a famous band. Now she felt the words catch. June might be here because she liked listening. Mara did not want her welcome to turn into another performance someone expected her to give.

Mara chose not to ask again. She placed the drum on the empty chair between them, not in front of June, and played the two soft beats once more. Then she left a full pause. The choice felt small and frightening. She could hear the trumpet group through it, but she did not fill it with a hint or a coaxing laugh.

June leaned forward. “Is that the whole pattern?” she asked.

“For now,” Mara said. “It can stay that way.” She slid the drum back to her own side. “You can just listen. Or go get a drink. You don’t owe the music anything.” June watched the blue ribbon settle. “I’d like to stay,” she said. “I’m not ready to play in front of people.”

Mara felt a quick sting of disappointment. She had wanted the duet, and the gathering was only an hour away. For a moment she imagined telling June that nobody would mind, that it was just one beat, that she could do it. Then she heard how each sentence would make June responsible for Mara’s hope.

“All right,” Mara said. “Listening it is.” She picked up the drum and played the phrase as quietly as she could. The loose edge made a soft, rounded sound. June followed the pauses. When Mara finished, June said, “It sounds a little like rain when you’re inside and the window is shut.”

Mara smiled. “That’s better than what I was thinking.” She played the phrase again, leaving the same roomy pause. June tapped one finger against her knee, almost too softly to hear. Mara noticed, but did not turn it into a question. She kept her eyes on the ribbon and finished the rhythm without changing its pace.

The trumpet group finally found its ending and packed away its stands. The room grew still enough for the ribbon to hang motionless. June looked up. “Could I try one beat now?” she asked. Mara nodded. “Only if you want to.” June moved her chair a few inches closer and put one hand on her knee.

Mara played the two-beat call, then stopped. June answered with a single tap on the drum’s rim. It was quieter than Mara’s, and it landed just after the beat rather than on it. Mara kept her hands still. June listened to the little sound fading. “I like that one,” she said. “It feels like mine.”

They tried the pattern once more, this time with June choosing where the pause went. They did not make it louder for the gathering or practice until it was perfect. The duet lasted only a few seconds. Still, it sounded complete to Mara: one person beginning, another answering, and both leaving room for silence afterward.

When the children’s circle ended, Mara tied the ribbon back into her pocket and carried the drum to Ms Vale. She told her about the warm sound near the rim. Ms Vale thanked her for returning it carefully and asked if June wanted to borrow one next time. June said, “Maybe. I’d like to hear how this one sounds again first.”

Mara stacked chairs beside June, not for her and not instead of her. At the door, June asked if Mara would be in the room next Saturday. “I usually am,” Mara said. They left the drum on its shelf and walked out together. The duet was over, but June had chosen its first beat, its last pause, and whether there would be another.
