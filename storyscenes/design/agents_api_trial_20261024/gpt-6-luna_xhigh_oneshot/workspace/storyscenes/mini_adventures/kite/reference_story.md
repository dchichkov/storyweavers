# The Blue Reel

All afternoon, Nia had been looking for a reason to knock on the blue gate next door. June had moved to Willow Lane two weeks ago. She waved when the grown-ups were watching, then disappeared whenever the children started a game. Nia wanted to fly her new kite with someone, not just show it off. She also wanted June to feel that the village had room for her.

The kite waited on the kitchen table, white paper stretched over two bamboo spars. Nia carried it to Tomas’s village kite workshop. Red and gold kites filled the rafters, and a blue wooden reel sat beside the door. Tomas was oiling a pair of scissors. “May I borrow that reel for the afternoon?” Nia asked. “The string on mine is almost gone.”

Tomas tested the reel’s handle with his thumb. “It’s my father’s. The line is strong, but it catches if you yank it. Bring it back wound neatly.” Nia promised. The reel was heavier than she expected. “Are you taking the big kite to the common?” Tomas asked. “I’m asking June to come.” “Good,” he said. “Ask her, don’t decide for her.”

June opened the gate only a hand’s width. “I’ve never flown a kite,” she said. Nia nearly answered that it was easy, then stopped. “You can choose when we let it go. We can stay away from the crowd.” June looked toward the busy green. “Just us?” Nia nodded. After a moment, June stepped out and shut the gate behind her.

At the workshop bench, June held the kite while Nia checked its frame. One spar had a fine split. The reel had enough line to lash the crack, but using it meant spending a little of what Tomas had lent. Nia hesitated. June touched the paper beside the spar, not the split itself. “It might hold if we tie it gently.”

Nia used three careful turns of string around the bamboo. The knot stayed put. They added a tail from blue cloth and checked that the paper did not sag. June asked if she could draw a small mark on the kite. Nia passed her the pencil. June made a neat blue star near the lower corner, then smiled at how little space it took.

Before leaving, June tied a loose ribbon to the workshop’s weather pole. It streamed out, then drooped, then streamed again. “The wind is changing,” she said. Nia had been thinking only about whether there would be wind at all. She listened to the ribbon, and the two of them carried the kite toward the common, taking the open path along the bakery wall.

The grass was still damp from morning rain. Nia held the reel; June held the tail. At Nia’s count, they ran. The kite lifted above the grass. A gust tugged the borrowed line sideways, and it looped over a low hawthorn twig. The kite hung crooked. June caught its tail before it dragged, while Nia felt the handle pull hard against her palms.

Nia could jerk the reel and try to wrench the line loose. It might save the kite quickly, but the string could snap—or the wooden handle could break. She remembered Tomas’s warning. June’s fingers were trembling on the tail, though she hadn’t let go. “Tell me what you need,” Nia said, instead of ordering her to run.

“Give me the handle,” June said. “Not all of it. Let me feel the pull.” Nia passed her the reel and took the kite’s tail. June waited for the ribbon’s lull, then fed out a little slack. Nia eased the twig free. Neither pulled alone. The kite dipped to the grass, with its frame intact and the line still whole.

They could have packed up. Instead, June looked at the ribbon and counted the breaths between its flutters. “Now,” she said. They ran together. June kept the reel steady while Nia guided the kite into the air. This time it climbed above the hawthorn and held. The blue star flashed when the kite turned. June’s laugh came out before she could hide it.

When the gusts grew stronger, they brought the kite down carefully. Nia wound in the loose string; June kept the tail from tangling around their ankles. They carried the reel back to Tomas’s workshop. “Every turn is neat,” he said, inspecting it. Nia explained how they had used a short length to mend the spar. Tomas examined the knot and nodded. “That’s what borrowed things are for, if you look after them.”

June wrote her name beneath the blue star. Nia added hers beside it. They hung the kite in the workshop window, where the afternoon light shone through its paper. June was still quiet when the village children passed, but now she stood beside Nia, holding the pencil. The kite had flown because they had trusted each other with its two ends—and the reel was back where it belonged.
