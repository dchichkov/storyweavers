# A Line Before Rain

At a quarter to four on Saturday, Nia was meant to be home putting the clean cups away. Instead, she was carrying an empty laundry basket through the shared garden. Her neighbour Mae had strained her thumb, and the washing beside the path was still waiting to be hung. Nia wanted to help before Mum called her in—and, if she was honest, wanted Mae to trust her with a proper job.

Mae sat by the shed, rubbing her hand. A pale sheet lay over the basket, with a shirt, a towel and a pair of socks tucked underneath. Above the fence, the sky was bright, but a grey bank had appeared over the flats. “That looks like a shower,” Nia said. “It does,” Mae answered. “And I can’t manage the sheet with this thumb.”

“Could I borrow your peg bag?” Nia asked. “I’ll finish it before the rain.” Mae studied her face, then handed over the canvas bag. “All right. There are eight pegs in there. Bring the bag back, even if the sky wins.” “I won’t lose it.” Nia liked that Mae had not said she was too young.

On the path, a snail was inching towards a puddle left by the watering can. Nia could have stepped around it and started straight away. Instead, she lifted it on a fallen leaf and set it beneath the marigolds. “You’re slower than the weather,” she told it. From the shed, Mae called, “That makes two of us.” Nia laughed and hurried on.

Mae came to the line with her good hand tucked into her cardigan pocket. “The sheet wants four corners,” she said. “Little things can share pegs, but not if they’re flapping.” Nia opened the bag and counted. She could hear a bicycle bell beyond the gate and, farther away, the low rumble of thunder.

“Corners first, corners last,” Mae said. “That’s my peg rhyme. It means check the sheet twice.” Nia repeated it, making the last word sound like a drumbeat. “You’ve been waiting all week to tell me that.” “Since Tuesday,” Mae replied. Her grin made the job feel less like a test.

The line had a little sag in it. Nia pressed it with one finger, then looked at the sheet. She could spend four pegs on its corners and use what remained on the smaller clothes. Or she could fill the line with little things first and leave the sheet for later. The sheet was the biggest thing, and the wind was picking up. “Corners first,” she decided.

Together they shook the sheet open. It billowed over Nia’s head and hid her from the garden for one breath. “I’ve disappeared,” she called. “I can still hear you,” Mae said. Nia caught a corner before it dragged through the damp soil. She fastened each corner carefully, leaving the final peg tight against a fold.

Nia hung the shirt and towel, then clipped the socks together. The bag was almost empty. She checked each peg with a small tug; the sheet held, though its middle lifted like a sail. “That’s the lot,” she said. Mae nodded, but her eyes were on the clouds, not the clothes. A gust made the line hum.

The first drops had not fallen, but the garden smelled suddenly of wet stone. Nia looked at the sheet. It was all on the line, as she had promised, yet the wind was worrying one corner. She could run to fetch an umbrella, call Mum, or get the washing under cover. Running home would waste the time she had borrowed with the peg bag.

“The shed rack is dry,” Mae said. “But the sheet is awkward, and I can’t lift it for you.” Nia felt the old urge to say she could do everything alone. Instead, she asked Mae to open the shed door while she handled the clothes. Mae’s thumb could manage a latch. “Good plan,” she said. “Tell me what you need.”

Nia unfastened the sheet and folded it once over her arm. The wind tugged at it, and one wet edge brushed her sleeve. Mae held the shed door while Nia carried the smaller things in two trips. They spread everything over the covered rack. It was not the neat line Nia had pictured, but no garment was left to catch the shower.

Rain rattled on the shed roof before Nia had finished smoothing the sheet. She glanced at the nearly empty peg bag, worried that Mae would think she had made a mess. Mae only pointed to the shirts. “Those are safe. And look—the sheet isn’t in the mud.” Nia let out the breath she had been holding.

“I didn’t beat it,” Nia said. “No,” Mae answered, “but you noticed what mattered and changed the plan.” She did not pretend that a sheltered rack was the same as a sunny line. Nia liked that. They had missed the first version of the job, but they had saved the washing together.

Nia collected the loose pegs and put them back in the bag. A few remained fastened to the rack; those were doing their work. “Here you are,” she said, handing it to Mae. “It held the sheet.” Mae took the bag with her unhurt hand. “And you brought it back useful.”

The shower softened. Nia remembered the snail and checked beneath the marigold leaves; it was tucked safely out of the puddle. Mae said she could finish putting away the cups after all, but Nia did not rush off. She helped fold the dry corner of the sheet and tucked the towel away from the shed door.

From the path came Mum’s call, not cross, just asking where Nia was. “Here,” Nia answered. “I’m coming.” Mae raised the peg bag. “Thank you for asking before taking it.” Nia thought of how easily she might have grabbed it and hurried. “Thank you for letting me try,” she said.

The garden looked different under the rain: darker leaves, bright drops on the line, and the shed roof ticking above them. Nia had not finished the washing in the way she first imagined. She had finished the more important part—nothing was abandoned, Mae’s sore thumb had been spared, and the borrowed bag was back where it belonged.

On Monday, the sheet would go out again when the clouds cleared. That was a real next job, not a promise Nia had already completed. For now, the clothes were safe under cover, Mae had her peg bag, and Nia went home with a damp sleeve and a warm feeling that did not need anyone to call her a hero.
