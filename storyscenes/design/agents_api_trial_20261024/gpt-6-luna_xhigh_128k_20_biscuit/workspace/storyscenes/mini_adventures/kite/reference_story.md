# The Last Dough

At five o’clock the kitchen was so busy that the kettle had to wait for a clear patch of counter. Mum stirred soup with one hand and answered the phone with the other. A tray of cooling buns occupied the table. Mara and her younger brother, Eli, had been told not to get underfoot, which was difficult when the last bowl of biscuit dough sat exactly between them.

“Mrs. Flores lent us her cutters,” Mara said, eyeing the star and bell shapes in a picture she had drawn on a scrap of paper. “We should make her a thank-you gift. A proper one.”

Eli scraped a bit of flour off his sleeve. “We should make biscuits we can eat, too. We both kneaded that dough.”

“It’s the last of it,” Mum called over the soup pot. “And I need this counter in ten minutes. Decide what you’re making, then clean up after yourselves.” She sounded brisk, not cross. Still, the warning made the dough feel suddenly smaller.

Mrs. Flores lived next door. She had lent them her old metal cutters that morning after Mara explained she wanted to make a surprise for her. They were narrow at the edges, with a little bent handle on the star. Mara had promised to bring them back clean. Eli had come along because he was good at remembering promises, though he said that wasn’t why.

Mara lifted the bowl. “We can ask if she’s home and borrow them now.”

“You already asked,” Eli said. “She said yes. That’s what lending means.”

Mara smiled despite herself. They hurried next door, knocked, and heard a chair scrape. Mrs. Flores opened the door with flour on her cheek. “You’re right on time,” she said. “I was wondering what you’d make with those.” She handed them the star and bell cutters in a tea towel. “Bring them back dry. I may bake tonight.”

Back in the kitchen, Mum had moved the bowl to a cooler corner. The dough was soft from being left near the stove. A jar of flour stood open beside it, but the counter was cluttered with Mum’s chopping board and a stack of clean plates. Mara found a narrow space and dusted it carefully.

Eli watched her. “You’re using all the clear space for the gift.”

“I’m using enough space to make it look nice.”

“And what do I get?”

“You get to help.”

“That isn’t a biscuit.” Eli’s voice was quiet, which made Mara look up. He had kneaded the dough while she drew the card. He wasn’t trying to spoil the surprise. He didn’t want their work to disappear into a box without either of them getting a say.

Mara’s first answer was ready: Mrs. Flores had helped them, and thank-you gifts should be generous. But Eli rubbed the small patch of flour on his sleeve, and she remembered their grandmother letting each child press a cutter into dough before she divided the tray. The cookies had never been identical. The sharing had been.

“We can measure it,” Mara said. “Exactly half for you and half for me.”

Eli frowned. “Then how does the gift get any?”

“Each of us gives half of our half. The gift gets the same amount from both of us. We keep the other half to share.”

He considered this. “And if one side is bigger?”

“Then we fix it.”

They split the last dough on the kitchen scale. The needle wavered, and Eli steadied the bowl while Mara pinched off a little piece. Once the two portions matched, each took half of theirs for the gift. Mum cleared a strip of counter without stopping her soup. “That looks fair,” she said, and set the oven to warm.

The dough was still soft. Mara sprinkled flour over the counter and Eli dusted the cutters. Their first star came away clean; the second held on to one point until Eli loosened it with a fingertip. They cut bells too. One bell leaned sideways, and Mara started to remake it, but Eli said, “It still rings in somebody’s head.” She let it stay.

While the oven warmed, Eli tapped a rhythm on the table with a wooden spoon. Mara joined in with the tea tin, softer so Mum could hear the timer. Their kitchen song had no words until Eli announced, “Flour on the table, bell in the tin.” Mara added, “Two good bakers, one small win.” They laughed and stopped before the timer rang.

The biscuits browned at their edges. One star lost a point, and a bell puffed until it looked like a mitten. Mara’s face tightened. Eli broke off a tiny browned corner and tasted it. “Still a biscuit,” he said. “Still a thank-you.” Mara tried one too. It was warm and sweet, even if it wasn’t the perfect row she had imagined.

They chose the two neatest stars and the straighter bells for Mrs. Flores. The rest went onto a plate for them. Mara folded the scrap of paper into a card, and Eli wrote the words because his letters were bigger: THANK YOU FOR LENDING THE CUTTERS. They tucked the biscuits into a small tin lined with clean paper. The gift was modest, but every piece had been made by both of them.

Before leaving, they washed the cutters together. Dough clung to the star’s bent handle, so Eli worked it loose with a soft brush while Mara dried each shape with a towel. She checked the edges before wrapping them. “They’ll be ready for her batch,” she said. Eli nodded, pleased that returning them properly was part of the gift too.

They split one warm biscuit at the counter, exactly down its middle. Mum glanced over her shoulder. “You saved me a crumb?” Eli placed a small piece beside the soup. “For quality control.” Mum laughed and ate it.

Next door, Mrs. Flores opened the tin and read the card. She turned the crooked bell in her hand. “This one’s my favorite,” she said. “It looks like it’s dancing.” Mara’s shoulders dropped. Eli grinned at her, but didn’t say he had been right. He didn’t need to.

Mara gave back the clean, dry cutters. Mrs. Flores set them beside a bowl of dough she had already prepared for her own evening baking. “Let’s see if your star still works,” she said, pressing it into the dough. It cut a clean shape. The borrowed tool had come home useful, not merely returned.

At the doorway, Mara thanked her for lending it. Eli thanked her for waiting while they made the card. On the walk back, neither carried the tin. It was empty, and that was how they knew the gift had reached its destination. In the kitchen, the last dough was gone, but their half of the promise had been kept together.
