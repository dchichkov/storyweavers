# The Place We Saved

On Friday afternoon, Mara and Kit were supposed to make lunch for their cousin Rowan. Rowan had promised to meet them by the river after fixing her bicycle, but she was always a little late when a wheel or a neighbor needed attention. Mara wanted the picnic to look cared for, not like something thrown together while they waited. Kit wanted Rowan to feel she could choose what happened, not just arrive in the middle of Mara’s plans. They agreed on rolls, apples, mint water, and a place on the blanket left open just for her.

Aunt Salma was hanging washing on the porch rail when Mara asked to borrow the blue blanket. “We’ll bring it back clean,” Mara said. Kit added, “And dry. You use it for the garden bench.” Aunt Salma looked toward the river. “The wind comes down the water without asking. Keep an eye on the corners.” She passed them the folded blanket, not as a warning exactly, but as someone lending a useful thing she expected to see again. Mara carried it carefully. Kit carried the basket.

The meadow was bright and damp at the edges. They chose a rise under a willow, far enough from the bank that nobody would slide into the water if the grass was slick. Mara shook out the blanket while Kit tucked one corner under a smooth stone. “A seat, not a sail,” Kit said. Mara laughed and left a space between them. She put the napkins there, then moved them when a twig fell. “Rowan gets the good spot,” she decided. Kit nodded, and pushed the basket into the shade.

Inside were small rolls, two apples, and a jar of mint water. Mara arranged the rolls like a shop display. One broke, scattering crumbs over the blue cloth. “That’s the crustiest bit,” Kit said, picking one up. “Rowan likes crust.” They saved the broken pieces and cut the apples into wedges. While Mara folded napkins, Kit found a hollow reed and tried to make a whistle. The first note sounded like a duck complaining. Rowan would have laughed at it, they agreed, so Kit set the reed beside the basket.

The gust came before they had finished pouring the water. It snapped the napkins up and lifted one blanket corner. Kit lunged for the basket; Mara caught the cloth. For a breath, both of them could not hold everything. “The rolls!” Kit shouted. Mara saw the blanket sliding toward the bank and remembered Aunt Salma’s careful hands smoothing its worn edge. She chose the blanket. Kit chose the basket a moment later, but one unlatched side tipped, and rolls bounced into the grass. The blanket stayed out of the river.

They stood still, listening to crumbs scratch across the cloth. Kit’s mouth tightened. “You picked Aunt Salma’s blanket over lunch.” “I picked the thing we promised to return,” Mara said, then heard how sharp it sounded. Kit had wanted Rowan to be welcomed with food, not an explanation. Mara admitted that was true. Together they gathered the rolls they could find. The damp grass had claimed most of them. Two apples remained, and the mint water was safe. Kit folded her arms, then helped Mara set the basket upright.

They waited. Kit played two crooked notes on the reed, then stopped when the sound made the empty place seem louder. A few crumbs lay on the blanket. Sparrows hopped out from beneath the willow and watched them. “They’re waiting too,” Kit said. She tipped the crumbs onto a flat stone. The birds pecked them up quickly and vanished into the reeds. Mara brushed the cloth with her palm. “We can tell Rowan what happened.” Kit answered, “We should. But don’t make it sound like the wind did all the choosing.”

Rowan appeared at last, pushing her bicycle with one hand. A trouser cuff was black with chain grease. “The chain came off by the bridge,” she said. “I thought you’d give up and go home.” Mara patted the empty place. “We saved your seat.” Kit offered her the mint water. Rowan looked at the two apples and the rescued rolls. “Did I miss the good part?” Kit told her about the gust, and about Mara choosing the blanket. Mara did not hide that she had been afraid Aunt Salma would be disappointed.

Rowan listened, then sat down on the grass instead of taking the blanket’s driest patch. “You kept the thing you borrowed, and you kept a place for me. That’s a decent lunch.” Kit cut the apples into three uneven pieces. Mara gave Rowan the largest wedge, but Rowan handed it to Kit, who had done the gathering. They shared the rescued rolls and the apple pieces. Kit told Rowan how long the wait had felt. Rowan apologized for not sending word; the cousins listened without making her apology the price of joining them.

When the food was gone, Mara and Kit carried the blanket home between them. They rinsed the grass marks and spread it over Aunt Salma’s sunny rail until it was clean and dry. Aunt Salma took it back and ran a thumb over the old blue stitching. “Ready for the garden bench again,” she said. Rowan stayed to help wind the mint jar’s string around the basket handle. Kit asked what Rowan would choose next time. “A place where all three of us can decide,” Rowan said. Mara agreed. They had not made the lunch they planned, but they had made room for the person it was meant for.
