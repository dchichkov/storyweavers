# The Boat at Willow Pond

At the little park pond, Jules’s blue wooden boat slipped from his hands when a gust flicked its string. It stopped beside a reed clump, its nose caught under a brown stem. The water there barely covered the path stones, but the middle was deeper. Jules leaned forward; Mara caught his sleeve. “Both feet stay on the bank.”

Jules blinked at her, then looked down at his shoes. “I wasn’t going in.” He was worried, not reckless. His grandfather had carved the hull from a fruit crate and painted a blue sail on it. The boat had been meant for a race in the rain gutter after school, not for a pond.

“I can reach it,” Mara said. She was the friend who usually carried the spare pencils and remembered whose turn it was. She wanted Jules to stop looking so miserable. She also wanted, just once, to solve a problem without being told she was hurrying.

The boat tugged at the stem each time the breeze returned. Jules held the loose string, but it had snapped near the bow. “If it goes under those reeds, I can’t see it.” Mara looked along the edge. “We’ll ask someone. We’re not going after it ourselves.”

Jules showed her the pencil initials under the stern. His grandfather had written them there before he died. “He let me steer it in the birdbath,” Jules said. “I promised I’d bring it home.” His voice went thin. Mara understood that the boat was more than a toy, even if it was small.

Near the park shed, Mr. Imani was coiling a green hose. Mara asked whether they could borrow the long-handled pond net. “We’ll both stay on the path,” she said. “We’ll bring it straight back.” Mr. Imani studied the children, then the pond. “That’s a sensible plan. You may borrow it while I finish this hose.”

The net’s wooden handle was longer than Mara’s arm span. Mr. Imani checked the dark mesh and ran a finger around the hoop. “No holes,” he said. “But don’t pull hard if you catch reeds. The handle can bend, and the bank edge is slippery.” Mara held the net upright, careful not to swing it near anyone.

Jules wanted to try immediately. Mara wanted to look first. They chalked a short line on the dry path. Mara knelt behind it; Jules stood a pace farther back, where he could watch the boat. “I’ll tell you if it moves,” he said. “And I won’t grab the handle.”

The reeds made a rough fringe along the pond. Jules pointed to a flat stone where two stems leaned away from each other. “There’s a gap. Granddad used to say you find the channel before you push the boat.” Mara traced the opening with her eyes. They were still on the dry bank; the clue was the water’s shape, not a reason to step closer.

A breeze rippled the pond and nudged the boat sideways. Mara tightened her hands on the handle. “Wait until it eases,” Jules said. He sounded calmer now that he had a job. They watched the leaves. When they stopped shivering for a moment, Mara moved the hoop toward the gap rather than straight at the boat.

The rim caught a reed. Mara felt the handle twitch. Her first thought was to yank, before the boat drifted farther away. Jules said, “Stop. That’s the stem, not the hull.” The net had snagged, but both children were balanced on the path. Mara let the handle go loose instead of turning a small problem into a broken net.

She eased the hoop backward a few centimetres. The reed bent, then slipped off. Mr. Imani’s warning came back to her: don’t pull hard. Jules watched the blue sail. “Try the opening again, a little lower.” Mara turned the hoop so its edge followed the clear patch. This time the mesh passed below the hull.

The boat rocked into the net. Mara could feel its weight at the end of the handle, light but unsteady. “I’ve got it,” she said. “Don’t reach for it.” Jules folded his hands against his own jacket. He wanted to help, but he understood that two sets of hands would make the narrow bank crowded.

Mara lifted slowly. Water streamed through the mesh, and the boat came over the stones. Its mast was bent, not broken. Jules waited behind the chalk line until Mara set the hoop down. Then he picked the boat up by its hull, the way his grandfather had shown him, and checked that the little keel still sat straight.

For a moment neither spoke. Jules rubbed the blue paint with his thumb. “It’s still a boat,” Mara said. “A bit of pond on it doesn’t make it a fish.” He laughed, short and relieved. The boat had not become new, but it could sail again in the gutter where they had planned to race it.

Mr. Imani was still by the shed. Mara carried the net back and explained that its rim had caught a reed before they found the opening. “We stopped pulling,” Jules added. Mr. Imani turned the hoop over, saw the mesh was whole, and nodded. “Good. That’s how you borrow something and leave it ready for the next person.”

Jules held the boat close while Mara handed over the net. She had wanted to be useful; now she felt useful in a quieter way. Mr. Imani accepted the handle, and Jules thanked him too. The net had done more than reach the water: it had let them bring the boat home without asking either child to risk a slippery step.

They walked to the park bench. Jules placed the boat on the slats, then straightened its sail with a careful finger. “You can steer the gutter race,” he said. “I’ll hold the start line.” Mara smiled. She had not needed to prove she could reach farther than the pond; she had needed to notice when reaching was not the same as helping.

They made a new string from Jules’s spare shoelace and tied it loosely to the bow. The boat would not be taken to the pond again. That was not a punishment; it was a better place for this little hull. Jules could still use it, and the next rain would fill the gutter outside his house.

When Mr. Imani called that the hose was ready, Mara and Jules waved goodbye. The blue boat rested safely in Jules’s hands, and the borrowed net was back in its place. They left the pond together, shoes dry. On the walk home, Jules began planning the race, and Mara let him choose the first course.
