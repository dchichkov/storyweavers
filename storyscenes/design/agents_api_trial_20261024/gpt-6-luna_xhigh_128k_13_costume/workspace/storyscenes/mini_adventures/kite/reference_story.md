# Before the Curtain: The Storm Costume

At three o’clock, the school hall smelled of dust, warm paint, and the oranges someone had packed for the cast. Ada had been asked to help in the costume cupboard because she remembered where everything belonged. She liked being trusted with the list and the keys. Jo came in carrying a folded script. “They promised me Storm,” she said. “I learned the lines on the bus.” On the rail, Jo found a gold tunic marked MOON. She tried it over her shirt; it pulled tight at the shoulders. Ada found a handwritten label underneath: JO—STORM, crossed out in blue pen.

“Maybe they changed the casting,” Ada said, though the page still listed Jo as Storm. Jo looked at the tunic’s narrow seam. “They didn’t tell me.” She wasn’t crying; she sounded much more annoyed than that. Ada knew the feeling. For weeks she had been trusted to cue the wind in the play, and she wanted the show to work exactly as rehearsed. “I can find Mrs Bell,” she said. Jo handed her the script. “Ask why first. Don’t ask them to give me some other part.” Ada nodded. She put the script on the counter, not in the pile marked returned.

A mirror leaned against a box of paper stars. Its glass was hazy with fingerprints. Ada wiped one clean patch with her sleeve, and Jo stood there holding up the gold tunic. In the mirror, the label on the rail was visible over Jo’s shoulder: STORM, then the blue line through her name. “I look like the Moon,” Jo said. “A very uncomfortable moon.” Ada laughed, then stopped. “You still want to play Storm?” “More than I want to wear this.” Jo’s answer was quick. Ada felt relieved that she didn’t have to guess what help meant.

On a nearby hook hung a long black cape from last year’s play. Ada borrowed it from the cupboard, careful to write her name on the return card. She held it out and Jo swept one corner through the air. It made a soft, convincing rush. “That’s the wind cue,” Ada said. “You could wear it.” Jo shook her head. “You need it for your cue. If you give it to me, you’re the one who loses something.” Ada had planned to carry it herself anyway. She tried it once more, this time making a gust that lifted the paper stars. Jo smiled despite herself.

A loose silver ribbon lay in the notions drawer. Jo tied it to the cape’s clasp, not to the tunic. “So the storm has a little moonlight,” she said. The detail was small, but it was her choice. Ada checked the clock. Mrs Bell was helping the younger class with their painted scenery, and the first audience would arrive soon. “I’ll go ask.” “Ask,” Jo repeated. “Not decide for me.” Ada promised she would come straight back with an answer, even if it was an answer neither of them liked.

Mrs Bell appeared in the doorway, holding a stack of scenery boards. Ada showed her the crossed-out label and asked about the storm costume. Mrs Bell frowned. “The right coat is still in the assembly hall. I sent someone for it, but it may not get here before places.” Jo didn’t hide behind Ada. “I was promised Storm,” she said. “The tunic hurts, and I’m not changing parts without being asked.” Mrs Bell apologized for the label, then hurried back to the hall. The promised coat might still come, but no one could make it arrive by wishing.

Ada and Jo examined the tunic. One seam had a little allowance, but the pin tin was empty. Ada began, “If we just—” Jo pulled the fabric away from her shoulder. “No pinning me into it. I want to be in the play, not pretend it fits.” Ada’s hands stopped. She had wanted to fix the problem quickly, before the cue list became late. Now she saw that a tidy costume was not the same as helping Jo. “Then we keep your part,” she said. “We change how the storm looks.” Jo looked at the cape, then at Ada. “Together?” “Together.”

They moved behind the side curtain to rehearse. Ada held the borrowed cape by its corners, and Jo stood on the other side. The work lamp threw their shapes onto a blank scenery cloth. Jo spoke her Storm lines from behind the moving black shape, deepening her voice on the thunder words. Ada swept the cape once, then waited for Jo’s cue instead of guessing. The silver ribbon flashed at the clasp. It was not the costume they had expected, but the wind had somewhere to go, and Jo’s voice filled the space.

When the play began, the Moon crossed the painted sky in its proper gold tunic. Then the stage went quiet. Jo’s voice said, “Listen—the weather is turning.” Ada raised the cape. Its shadow rolled across the scenery, and the silver ribbon caught the work light like a thin lightning streak. Jo spoke the Storm’s lines, clear and steady. From the front row, Mrs Bell watched with both hands clasped. Ada remembered to let Jo’s words lead the wind.

After the final bow, Jo came offstage grinning. “That was Storm,” she said. “It was,” Ada answered. Mrs Bell apologized again, this time without making an excuse, and promised to correct the cast list before the next performance. Ada carried the cape back to its hook; it had done real work for the scene, and she returned it with the ribbon safely untied. Jo put the script in her own bag. “Next time,” she said, “we check the label together.” Ada handed her the silver ribbon to keep. The curtain was down, but Jo had not been made smaller to fit the wrong costume.
