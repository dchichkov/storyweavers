# The Line Between Us

By nine on Saturday, the village square had six bare posts and one paper notice that said WELCOME in letters too small to read from the road. Leni, who had volunteered to decorate it, wanted bunting stretched high across the arch. “People should see it before they turn the corner.” Mrs Bell was setting out folding tables for the festival. New neighbour Jo had moved into the green cottage two days ago, and Leni wanted the welcome to feel like a real welcome, not a notice pinned to a door.

The bunting lay in a basket, but its string had run out. Leni found Mrs Bell coiling a red-and-white spool by the village hall. “Could I borrow it? I’ll bring it back after we hang everything.” Mrs Bell passed it over. “The spool, please. The string you use can stay where it’s useful.” Leni promised, and carefully put the spool in her canvas bag. It was heavier than she expected; she liked that it felt like a proper job.

Jo was sitting on the square bench with one ankle in a walking boot and a cane leaning against the seat. Leni had seen them arrive with two boxes and a plant. “You’re the new neighbour,” she said, then immediately wished she had found a better opening. Jo laughed. “That’s what the boxes say.” Leni explained the festival and the bare posts. Jo listened, then asked, “Is there something I can do, or am I just here to be welcomed?”

“We can hang the bunting on the arch,” Leni said. “I could climb the low side and pass the string over.” Jo’s smile disappeared. “Please don’t. My ankle can’t climb, and I don’t want your festival to start with a fall.” Leni looked up at the arch. It seemed a long way up now. “I thought I was making it grand.” “Grand is fine,” Jo said. “Being able to help is better.” Leni heard the difference between doing the job for Jo and doing it with Jo.

They examined the square instead of the arch. Two waist-high posts stood near the path; Jo could reach them from the bench, and Leni tested each one with a careful tug. One post wobbled. The other pair, by the low garden wall, held firm. Jo picked up two paper triangles. “Blue and gold,” they said. “My old harbor town used those colors on its lanterns.” Leni let Jo arrange the pieces. The pattern looked brighter than the red-and-white basket, and Jo’s hands were already part of the decoration.

Leni made a loop in the borrowed string and pulled. It slid loose. “This spool is no good,” she muttered. Jo turned the string between their fingers. “It’s smooth, not bad. Try a second loop.” They practiced on a short tail, using a little of the string, then leaned back together. The knot held against the basket handle. “A knot you trust is better than a ladder,” Jo said. Leni tucked the practice piece away instead of pretending it had never failed.

Clouds hurried over the rooftops. A gust lifted the paper triangles, and a cold drop struck Leni’s cheek. The low posts would be wet soon; the square was exposed, and the unsteady pair would not hold a line. The tall arch was still there, but it would need climbing and the wind could tug at anyone up high. “We could wait,” Leni said. Jo looked toward the village hall and its deep porch. “Or we can welcome people under a roof now.” Leni considered the posters already sent around the village. Moving the display meant giving up her grand arch, but not the festival.

They carried the basket and spool to the hall rail, where the roof kept the boards dry. The rail was low enough for Jo to reach without standing, and Leni measured a shorter span than the outdoor line would have needed. “Room for six triangles,” she said. “Seven,” Jo answered, sliding the blue and gold one to the middle. Leni tied each end. Jo made the final double loop, then tugged until it held. Nobody climbed; nobody had to watch from the side. The string had turned a bare rail into a welcome.

Mrs Bell opened the hall doors as the first neighbours arrived. Leni stood by the bunting. “We had a bigger plan,” she said, “but this is the one we could make together.” Jo pointed to the blue-and-gold triangles. “They’re from my old town. This one is my favourite.” An older couple stopped to ask about the colors. Jo told them about the harbor lanterns, and they stayed to help arrange cups. Leni noticed that people were gathering around Jo, not merely being directed toward them.

The rain pattered on the porch while the festival filled the hall. Children followed the low line to the tables; a little girl reached up to straighten a triangle, and Jo showed her how to tie the corner securely. When the music started, Leni danced with Mrs Bell between the chairs while Jo kept time with a spoon against a mug. The arch outside remained bare. Leni saw it through the window and was surprised to find she did not mind. The welcome was happening here, at the height where Jo could join it.

When the last cups were collected, Leni carried Mrs Bell’s spool back to the hall cupboard. The remaining string was wound neatly around it; the used length still held the bunting in place. “You put it to work,” Mrs Bell said. Jo had already promised to show the neighbors the harbor pattern another day, and now there were three people asking to learn the knot. Leni was glad she had returned the spool with something useful left on it, not just an apology for borrowing it.

On the way home, Jo walked slowly beside Leni, cane tapping the dry edge of the path. “I thought moving here would mean starting every story from the beginning,” Jo said. Leni pointed back at the lit hall. “We’ve started one.” Behind them, the low bunting stirred gently whenever the door opened. It had not reached the highest place in the square, but it had made room for a new neighbor to hold one end—and for everyone else to come closer.
