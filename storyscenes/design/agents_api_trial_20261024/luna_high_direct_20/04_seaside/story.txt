The wind had been stealing Tessa’s sketchbook all morning. It flipped the pages while she tried to draw the sea, and blew her pencils off the garden wall. Her brother Milo wanted to find a tide pool where he could watch Captain, a green anemone they’d seen last summer, open and close like a tiny flower.

“Fern Pocket is sheltered,” Mr. Vale told them, handing over his folded map. “Follow the seaside path to the Twin Chimneys, then turn inland at the stile. The real chimneys are two dark rocks out in the water. Don’t mix them up with the old bathhouse.”

“We won’t,” Tessa said.

Milo tucked the picnic basket under one arm. Tessa slid the map into her jacket. Mr. Vale watched them set off along the seaside path, then called, “The tide turns at two!”

“We’ll be back before then!” Milo called.

The path wound above a stony beach, with the sea flashing between clumps of grass. Tessa kept one hand on the map and the other on her sketchbook. Milo walked ahead, looking for pools left by the last tide.

At a fork, Tessa stopped. Below them stood the old bathhouse, its pale roof almost hidden behind a dune. Two chimneys stuck up side by side.

“There,” she said. “Twin Chimneys.”

Milo peered at the map. “Mr. Vale said the rocks are out in the water.”

“Maybe the rocks are behind the bathhouse,” Tessa replied. The lower path led straight toward it, while the upper one curved away from the sea. “This must be the shortcut.”

Milo hesitated, then followed her down.

The bathhouse was locked and boarded up. Beyond it, the path ended on a narrow shelf of pebbles. Tessa took out the map. The drawing showed two dark stacks with little waves around them. On the far side of the point, she could just make out the actual Twin Chimneys rising from the water.

“Oh,” she said.

“We followed the bathhouse,” Milo said. He didn’t sound cross, but he was looking at the tide pool below the pebbles. “Do you think Captain might be in there?”

Tessa checked the map again. The dotted line to Fern Pocket ran along the upper path, not down here. She had been so sure she’d spotted the landmark that she hadn’t looked closely.

A wave slid over the flat rocks at the end of the shelf. It withdrew with a hiss, leaving the stones shining.

“We can scramble around the point,” Tessa said, though the map showed no path there. Fern Pocket was so close. She pictured her sketchbook resting flat in the quiet, with the sea still in view.

Milo pointed to the water creeping across the rocks. “That way’s getting smaller.”

The old stone steps back to the fork were steep but dry. They could climb them and take the upper path. It would mean going past the place they’d just left, and Tessa would have to admit she’d led them the wrong way. She looked at the basket, then at the moving water.

“Up the steps,” she said. “We’re not going around the point.”

Milo nodded. He lifted the basket, and together they climbed. At the top, Tessa stopped to catch her breath.

“I should’ve checked the map,” she said.

“You did check it,” Milo replied. “You just checked it after choosing.”

She gave him a look. He grinned, and she couldn’t help laughing.

From the upper path, the real Twin Chimneys stood plain against the water. Tessa followed their dark shapes on the map, then spotted the wooden stile just beyond the bend. This time, she let Milo read the directions.

“Over the stile,” he said. “Then the path goes between the rocks.”

He was right. Beyond the stile, the path slipped inland, where the wind softened among the grasses. Ferns crowded a small hollow under the headland. A low stone wall made a perfect place to set down the basket, and the sea was still visible through a gap in the rocks.

Tessa opened her sketchbook. The pages stayed still.

Milo hurried to the nearest tide pool and crouched. “Captain!” A green anemone opened slowly in the clear water. Milo kept his hands on his knees and watched it bloom.

Tessa settled beside him. “I can see both chimneys from here,” she said. “Hold still a second.”

While she drew, Milo unfolded the map across the picnic cloth and used two apples to keep its corners down. He traced their route with one finger, from the wrong turn to the high path, then pointed to the sheltered hollow where they sat.

When the wind lifted the map’s edge, Milo pressed it flat with his palm. Tessa’s pencil moved across the page, drawing two dark rocks in the sea and, below them, a small green flower opening in its pool.
