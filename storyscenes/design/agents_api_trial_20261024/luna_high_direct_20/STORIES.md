## 01_bakery | Seed 2026102402 | 852 words

Tessa had saved nine copper coins in a blue jar, and on Thursday morning she carried them to Pike’s Bakery.

“I’d like a small birthday cake,” she told Mrs. Pike. “For Mr. Hobb.”

Mrs. Pike paused with her rolling pin in the air. “The one at the end of Mill Lane?”

“He mended my kite last spring,” said Tessa. “While he was fixing it, he told me his birthday was today. He said nobody had remembered for years.”

She set her coins on the counter. They made a small, clinking pile.

Mrs. Pike counted them, then slid two back. “That buys a little cake and one candle.”

The cake was round, with lemon icing and a blue border. Mrs. Pike wrote HAPPY BIRTHDAY, MR. HOBB across the top. The letters were neat, though the final B leaned into the icing flower beside it.

“Can I borrow a delivery basket?” Tessa asked. “I want to carry it carefully.”

Mrs. Pike found a broad wicker basket and laid a folded tea towel inside. “Keep it level. The stones on Mill Lane are uneven, and this basket has a habit of tipping if you rush.”

“I won’t rush.”

“Bring it back tomorrow. I need it for the morning loaves.”

Tessa tucked the cake into the basket and held both handles. She walked slowly past the green, past the well, and up the lane. At the old bridge she stopped. The short path over it was muddy from last night’s rain. She could see the long paved way winding around the hill.

The long way would take half an hour more. Tessa glanced at the cake. The blue icing shone through its paper cover.

She turned toward the paved way.

Even there, the stones were lumpy. At the steepest part of the hill, a cart rattled past, and its wheels jolted the road. The basket bumped against Tessa’s knee. She froze, holding it level until the cart had clattered away.

When she lifted the paper, one corner of the cake had shifted against the box. The icing flower was smudged, and the last B had become a blue squiggle.

Tessa pressed the box gently back into place. The cake was still whole.

At last she reached Mr. Hobb’s cottage. The curtains were shut, though a thin line of lamplight showed beneath them. Tessa knocked.

Nothing.

She knocked again, louder. “Mr. Hobb? It’s Tessa.”

A floorboard creaked inside. Then all was quiet.

Tessa looked at the basket. Mrs. Pike needed it tomorrow. She could leave the cake on the doorstep, hurry home, and return the basket before dark. But the drizzle was starting again, and a cake left outside would not stay whole for long.

She knocked a third time.

“I don’t want a cake,” called a voice from inside.

“I know,” Tessa called back. “I want you to have one.”

The door opened a crack. Mr. Hobb peered out, his gray hair sticking up at the back. “Who told you it was my birthday?”

“You did. When you fixed my kite. You said it was the day the swallows came back.”

Mr. Hobb looked at her for a long moment. Then he opened the door wider. “I remember that kite. It had a tail made of old socks.”

“They were good socks,” said Tessa.

“They were very colorful socks.”

She carried the basket inside. Mr. Hobb cleared a place at the small kitchen table, moving a stack of newspapers and a single mug. Tessa lifted the cake box out of the basket. The cake had tilted a little, but it had not fallen apart.

Mr. Hobb studied the icing. “That flower seems to be swimming.”

“It got bumped on the hill.”

“And the last letter has gone wandering.” He smiled—not a big smile, but a real one. “Still, it’s a fine cake.”

Tessa set the candle in the center. Mr. Hobb found a match in a drawer. Its flame trembled as he lit the wick.

“You should make a wish,” Tessa said.

“I’m too old for wishes.”

“You’re exactly the right age for one. It’s your birthday.”

Mr. Hobb lowered his head and blew. The candle went out on the first try. When he switched on the kitchen light, he opened the curtains, and the window turned into a bright square in the rainy evening.

He cut the cake. The slices came out uneven: one tall, one thin, and one with the whole icing flower on top.

“Take the flower,” he said.

“I paid for the cake,” Tessa replied. “You get the flower.”

So he did. They ate at the little table while the rain ticked softly at the glass. The wicker basket sat beside Tessa’s chair, empty now, with the tea towel folded neatly inside.

When she left, Mr. Hobb walked her to the gate. “I’ll bring the basket back to the bakery tomorrow,” he said.

“Mrs. Pike said I should.”

“Then we’ll both bring it.”

Behind him, the kitchen window glowed warm and gold. On the table, the blue-iced cake stood between two plates, its candle laid beside it like a tiny silver twig.

## 02_puppets | Seed 2026102403 | 840 words

The puppet theatre at Cedar School was no wider than a closet, but when its red curtain closed, the cardboard moon looked enormous.

At three o’clock, Nia was checking every cue on her list, Ben was trying to make the paper clouds sway, and Lila was crouched behind the little stage with Mouse on her hand. Nia wanted to finish the rehearsal before the caretaker locked the hall. Ben wanted the moon scene to look as beautiful as the scenery he had painted. Lila wanted the final scene to go perfectly without anyone noticing her.

“Mouse brings the star,” Nia read. “Then the Moon says—”

Lila reached into Mouse’s tiny pocket. Her fingers found only the lining.

“The star’s gone,” she whispered.

It was a silver felt star, no bigger than a biscuit, with a loop of blue thread at its tip. Mouse was supposed to give it to the Moon, who had been lonely in the dark. Lila had made the star herself and had carried it to school in the prop tin.

“Where did you last see it?” Ben asked.

“On the windowsill at lunch. I took it out to fix the loop.” Lila swallowed. “Then the bell rang.”

They searched the windowsill, the radiator, the prop tin, and the floor beneath the stage. Ben even lifted the cardboard clouds, though he had glued them together that morning. No star.

Nia glanced at her list. “The class is coming in five minutes.”

“We can skip the scene,” Lila said. She kept Mouse’s face turned toward the floor. “It won’t matter.”

“It’s the ending,” Ben said. “The Moon can’t be lonely forever.”

Nia shut her list. “There’s a sewing kit in Ms. Rowan’s costume cupboard. Come on.”

Ms. Rowan was sorting a box of old scarves. When Nia explained, she handed over a green sewing tin and a square of gray felt.

“Bring it back with the needle,” she said. “And don’t cut the curtain to make a star.”

“We promise,” Ben said.

Behind the stage, Nia held the felt while Ben rummaged through the tin. Lila found a brass button with a small flower stamped on it.

“We could sew this in the middle,” she said.

“It’s not silver,” Ben pointed out.

“The Moon can have a golden star,” Nia said. “It might even show better.”

Lila turned the button over in her palm. “I don’t want it to look like we forgot the real one.”

“We didn’t forget,” said Nia. “We lost it.”

That made Lila smile, just a little.

They folded the felt and cut a lopsided star. Nia threaded the needle, but the thread frayed twice. Ben held the felt tight while Lila pushed the needle through. The button was heavier than it looked. When they lifted their new star, its point sagged down.

“It’ll flop over,” Ben said.

Lila studied Mouse’s pocket. “Then it shouldn’t go in the pocket. Mouse can hold it up for the Moon to see.”

“But the Moon is meant to keep it,” Nia said.

“Mouse can tuck it into the Moon’s lap,” Lila replied. “The button will face the audience.”

She stitched a loop of thread onto the star’s top so Mouse could lift it without dropping it. Her first knot came loose. She tried again, pulling the thread tight this time.

They tested the scene. Mouse raised the star. It swung sideways, and the button flashed toward the wall.

“Wait,” Lila said. She turned the star around and tied the loop shorter. “Now.”

Mouse lifted it again. The gold button faced the theatre.

Nia nodded. “That works.”

The class filed into the little theatre, whispering and bumping knees. Ben slipped behind the cardboard scenery. Nia took her place beside the curtain with her cue list. Lila held Mouse beneath the stage, her hands trembling enough to make his ears quiver.

The rehearsal began. Mouse crossed the cardboard forest. The Moon rose above the painted hills.

Then came the final scene.

“Mouse brings the star,” Nia read.

Lila lifted Mouse. For a moment, her voice stayed trapped in her throat. She could hear the room waiting. The old silver star was gone. This one was crooked, gray, and bright with a brass button.

She could let Nia read the line instead. Or she could make the scene her own.

Lila drew a breath. “I brought you a little light,” she said—not in a whisper, but in Mouse’s clear, small voice.

The class leaned forward. Mouse offered the star. The Moon held out both felt hands, and Lila settled the star in its lap.

“I can see my way now,” Nia read as the Moon.

Ben moved the paper clouds. The curtain began to close.

Afterward, Nia returned the sewing tin to Ms. Rowan, with the needle tucked safely inside. Lila kept Mouse on her hand as the three friends stood in the empty theatre. Behind them, the red curtain met in the middle. In the dimness, the brass button on the new star caught one last dot of light.

By three o’clock, the community greenhouse felt like a glass oven. Sunlight poured through the roof and turned the air warm and green.

Nia came in carrying a yellow umbrella and a pot with one sunflower seedling. She wanted to take the seedling home to her grandmother’s porch, but first she needed to choose which of its two new leaves looked strongest.

Then she saw the tomato seedlings on the far bench.

Their leaves drooped over the edges of their trays. A label read: MRS. OKAFOR’S TOMATOES — PLEASE DON’T MOVE.

“They look thirsty,” Nia said.

Mr. Bell, the greenhouse caretaker, was crouched beside the hose tap. He twisted it, frowned at the dry spout, and tried again.

“I’ve shut off the water while I fix a leak,” he said. “The rain barrel has enough for one watering can. After that, we wait.”

“Could I borrow your blue can?” Nia asked. “I want to water Mrs. Okafor’s seedlings.”

Mr. Bell glanced at the tomato trays. “All right. Fill it from the barrel, and pour gently at the soil. Don’t move the trays. Mrs. Okafor has them sorted by variety.”

Nia filled the can. It was heavier than she expected, and water sloshed against the sides as she carried it to the bench. She crouched and tipped the rose over the first tray.

A soft sprinkle darkened the soil around each tiny stem. Nia moved slowly, row by row, until the can grew light. She had just enough for the tomatoes.

Then a flap of shade cloth above the bench lifted with a dry snap. One of its clips had broken. The cloth hung crooked, leaving the nearest tray in a bright square of sun.

Nia looked at Mr. Bell. He was under the sink now, reaching for a wrench. The hose still wouldn’t work.

She looked at her sunflower. Its pot sat beside her knee, and its soil was dusty too. She could have saved a little water for it. Instead, she had emptied the can on the tomatoes.

The yellow umbrella leaned against her backpack.

Nia opened it and held it over the exposed tray. Its shadow covered the seedlings, but the handle was awkward in her hand, and she couldn’t keep holding it while she helped Mr. Bell. She scanned the bench. An empty clay pot stood nearby, heavy with pebbles.

Nia set the umbrella handle into the pot and tilted the open canopy toward the sun. A breath of air nudged it sideways.

“Stay,” she told it.

She found a length of soft garden tie in the drawer and looped it around the umbrella shaft and the bench leg. The umbrella held, though it leaned at a jaunty angle. Its shadow stretched across the tomato tray and reached the edge of Nia’s sunflower pot.

Mr. Bell slid out from beneath the sink. “That should buy us some time,” he said. He gave the umbrella a careful tap. “Good thinking.”

Nia lifted the blue can. “Can I refill it when the hose is fixed?”

“If I can get this leak stopped.”

A few minutes later, the greenhouse door creaked open. Mrs. Okafor came in carrying a paper bag of clothespins. She stopped when she saw the umbrella.

“My shade cloth slipped,” she said, hurrying over. “I went to find clips.”

“It snapped,” Nia explained. “The umbrella covers the sunny tray.”

Mrs. Okafor checked the damp soil with one finger, then examined the crooked shade. “And you watered at the roots. Just right.” She smiled at Nia. “Would you help me put the cloth back?”

Together they stretched the shade cloth across its rail. Mrs. Okafor fastened one end with a clothespin while Nia held the other steady. Mr. Bell came over with a new metal clip from his toolbox and secured the broken corner.

The umbrella had done its job. Nia shook the tie loose and closed it. Her sunflower’s leaves had stopped drooping in the patch of shade. Its soil was still dry, but Mr. Bell had just fixed the tap.

“Your can’s ready,” he said.

Nia filled it again and watered her sunflower, careful not to splash its leaves. Then she returned the blue can to Mr. Bell.

Before leaving, Mrs. Okafor straightened the label on her tomato tray. Beneath the repaired shade, the smallest seedling lifted its first true leaf. A bead of water trembled at its tip, bright as a little glass bell.

“We won’t,” Tessa said.

“We’ll be back before then!” Milo called.

“There,” she said. “Twin Chimneys.”

Milo peered at the map. “Mr. Vale said the rocks are out in the water.”

“Maybe the rocks are behind the bathhouse,” Tessa replied. The lower path led straight toward it, while the upper one curved away from the sea. “This must be the shortcut.”

Milo hesitated, then followed her down.

The bathhouse was locked and boarded up. Beyond it, the path ended on a narrow shelf of pebbles. Tessa took out the map. The drawing showed two dark stacks with little waves around them. On the far side of the point, she could just make out the actual Twin Chimneys rising from the water.

“Oh,” she said.

A wave slid over the flat rocks at the end of the shelf. It withdrew with a hiss, leaving the stones shining.

Milo pointed to the water creeping across the rocks. “That way’s getting smaller.”

“Up the steps,” she said. “We’re not going around the point.”

Milo nodded. He lifted the basket, and together they climbed. At the top, Tessa stopped to catch her breath.

Milo hurried to the nearest tide pool and crouched. “Captain!” A green anemone opened slowly in the clear water. Milo kept his hands on his knees and watched it bloom.

Tessa settled beside him. “I can see both chimneys from here,” she said. “Hold still a second.”

While she drew, Milo unfolded the map across the picnic cloth and used two apples to keep its corners down. He traced their route with one finger, from the wrong turn to the high path, then pointed to the sheltered hollow where they sat.

When the wind lifted the map’s edge, Milo pressed it flat with his palm. Tessa’s pencil moved across the page, drawing two dark rocks in the sea and, below them, a small green flower opening in its pool.
