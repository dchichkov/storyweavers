At four o’clock, the family kitchen was loud enough to rattle the spoons in their jar. Mom stirred tomato sauce, Dad peeled potatoes, and Grandma sat at the table shelling peas into a bowl. At the end of the counter, Mara and her little brother, Toby, had flour on their sleeves and biscuit dough under their fingernails.

“Careful with my cutters,” Grandma said, sliding over her tin. “Borrow them, don’t bend them.”

Inside were a star, a rabbit, a flower, and a dog with one floppy ear. Mara chose the star. Toby grabbed the dog.

“We’re making these for Mr. Alvarez,” Mara reminded him. “He likes neat things.”

“He likes Pepper,” said Toby. “The dog one’s for Pepper.”

Mr. Alvarez had found their terrier, Pepper, two nights earlier. She’d wriggled out of her collar during a thunderstorm. While Mara and Toby searched the block with their parents, Mr. Alvarez had followed muddy pawprints all the way to the laundromat, holding his yellow flashlight low to the ground. He’d brought Pepper home wrapped in his raincoat.

Mom had offered to make a thank-you cake, but Mara and Toby wanted to do it themselves. They’d borrowed Grandma’s cutters, and Mom had given them an empty blue tin for the finished biscuits.

Mara rolled the dough smooth. Toby sprinkled flour, sneezing so hard that Grandma’s peas jumped in their bowl. They cut a row of stars and a row of dogs, then gathered the scraps and rolled them again.

The kitchen timer chimed. Mom glanced at the oven. “Your tray goes in now. It needs eight minutes, then I need the oven for supper.”

Mara looked at the dough left on the board. One lump, about as big as Toby’s fist. It was enough for one more biscuit.

“I want one last star,” she said. “Then the tin will look finished.”

Toby put his hand over the lump. “We need another dog. Mr. Alvarez found Pepper.”

“He already has four dogs.”

“But not one like Pepper.”

“He didn’t find her because she was shaped like a dog,” Mara said.

Toby tugged the dough toward the dog cutter. Mara caught the other side. The lump stretched, then split with a soft rip.

They stared at the two uneven pieces.

“Now it’s not even big enough for either one,” Mara said.

Toby’s ears went pink. “I was trying to save the best piece.”

“For your idea,” Mara said.

Dad stopped peeling. Mom kept stirring, but her eyes moved to the children. Grandma set down her pea pod. No one reached for the dough.

The timer ticked on.

Mara picked up the larger piece. She pressed the torn edges together with Toby’s smaller piece, then dusted the board with flour. “We can still make one.”

“A star?” Toby asked.

“A dog,” Mara said. “But you can help.”

Toby’s face brightened. He guided the dog cutter while Mara pressed it down. Then he held the star cutter above the dog’s back.

“Don’t cut all the way through,” Mara said.

“I know.”

He pressed gently, leaving a star-shaped mark in the dough. Mara sprinkled sugar into the little grooves. Together they lifted the odd-shaped biscuit onto the tray. There was just room for it between a star and a rabbit.

The tray went into the oven with one minute to spare. While the biscuits baked, Mara washed Grandma’s cutters, and Toby dried each one carefully with a dish towel. When the timer rang, Mom slid the supper dish into the oven. The kitchen filled with the warm smell of butter and sugar.

They tucked the cooled biscuits into the blue tin, laying the dog with the star on top. Then they carried it next door.

Mr. Alvarez opened the door with his yellow flashlight clipped to his belt. Pepper squeezed past his legs and wagged so hard her whole back end wobbled.

“For me?” he asked, taking the tin.

“And for Pepper,” Toby said.

Mara pointed to the top biscuit. “That’s a dog, and the star is for your flashlight.”

Mr. Alvarez laughed softly. “I remember that night. I could only see the pawprints when I held the light close.”

He set the tin on the hallway table and lifted the special biscuit. The porch light caught the little sugar-star pressed into its back, bright against the browned dough.
