By nine o’clock, the fruit market smelled of orange peel, damp crates, and the cinnamon buns from the bakery stall. Mina stood behind her aunt Farah’s apples, polishing a red one with her sleeve.

“I can do Mrs. Dutta’s order,” she said. “All by myself.”

Aunt Farah glanced at the handwritten list. “Two kilos of tart apples, for her grandson’s birthday pie. Our scale is at the repair shop, so you’ll need to borrow Mr. Boulos’s.”

Mina’s grin widened. Aunt Farah had let her stack fruit and make change, but never fill an order alone. “I’ll bring it back clean.”

At the next stall, Mr. Boulos was arranging jars of olives. His brass scale sat beside them, its round dial shining.

“May I borrow your scale, please?” Mina asked.

“Certainly. Mind the pan; it wobbles if you bump the counter.” He lifted it over and set it in her hands. “And bring it back before I sell my last olive.”

Mina carried it carefully to the apple stall. Mrs. Dutta, their next-door neighbor at home, was waiting with a cloth shopping bag folded over her arm.

“I have to catch the eleven-twenty bus,” she said. “My grandson arrives tonight, and he’s already told everyone he expects pie.”

“I’ll be quick,” Mina promised.

She chose firm, speckled apples and set them one by one on the brass pan. The needle climbed: one kilo, one and a half, then two. Mina slid the apples into a paper bag.

“Exactly two kilos,” she said.

Mrs. Dutta reached for the bag, but Mina turned to return the scale first. Its pan was empty. The needle still pointed at a quarter kilo.

Mina frowned. She lifted the pan. A curled green leaf was pinched beneath its rim, pressing the scale down. She plucked it free. The needle sprang back to zero.

“Wait,” she said.

Mrs. Dutta paused, her fingers on the bag’s handles. “What is it?”

Mina put the bag back on the pan. The needle stopped at one and three-quarter kilos.

For a moment, the market’s bustle seemed to hush around her. Mina could hand over the bag and no one would know. Mrs. Dutta would be on her bus, and Aunt Farah would think she’d done the job perfectly.

Instead, Mina pushed the bag gently away from Mrs. Dutta. “I’m sorry. The leaf made the scale say there was more than there was. You’re short a quarter kilo.”

Mrs. Dutta looked at the dial, then at Mina. “Can you fix it before my bus?”

“I can try.” Mina’s cheeks felt hot. “I’ll weigh the apples again, and I’ll tell Aunt Farah what happened.”

She emptied the bag into a crate and started over, checking the dial between each handful. This time it took three small apples to bring the needle to exactly two kilos. She folded the top of the bag and tied it with twine.

Aunt Farah came back carrying an empty crate just as Mrs. Dutta checked the time.

“What happened?” she asked.

Mina showed her the curled leaf. “I thought the order was right, but it wasn’t. I stopped before she took it.”

Aunt Farah examined the scale and nodded. “Good catch. Mr. Boulos should know, too.”

“I’ll tell him,” Mina said. “And I’ll return the scale.”

Mrs. Dutta tucked the bag into her cloth shopping bag. “I’ll still catch my bus. And you’ve saved my pie from being a little short on apples.”

She left a coin on the counter for the order and hurried toward the market gate. Mina watched until she turned the corner, then carried the scale back to Mr. Boulos.

“I found a leaf under the pan,” she told him. “It was making the dial read too high. I cleaned it and checked it again.”

Mr. Boulos turned the scale over, then gave Mina a serious nod. “Thank you for telling me. You may borrow it again whenever you need it.”

By closing time, Aunt Farah had put Mina’s name at the top of tomorrow’s order list. When Mrs. Dutta returned with a warm slice of birthday pie wrapped in a napkin, Mina set it beside the scale. The dial rested neatly on zero, and the pie’s golden crust glowed in the last square of sunlight.
