The orchard was small enough for Tessa to hear Jun calling from one end while she stood at the other. Pear trees leaned over the grass, their leaves flashing silver whenever the breeze turned them. Jun had brought two oatcakes and a wedge of cheese. Tessa had brought a little jar of honey.

“Three pears,” Jun said. “One for Willa, and one each for us.”

His little sister had promised to hold a puppet picnic that evening. Jun had promised to bring the fruit.

“And I want to pick them myself,” Tessa said. Her cousin usually took over whenever there was a tool to use. “Carefully.”

Mrs. Bell, who owned the orchard, listened from beside the shed. “Then you may borrow my picking basket.” She lifted a long handle with a cloth pouch and a ring of soft-tipped fingers at the end. “Stand on the ground. Slip the ring around a pear, then turn gently. If it won’t come, leave it. No climbing, shaking, or yanking.”

She pointed to an empty wooden crate near the gate. “You can set the basket there when you’re finished. It’s not a step.”

Jun glanced at the crate. “I know.”

Tessa took the handle, and they walked to the nearest tree. Its low branches held small green pears and a few that had turned golden. She fitted the ring around one of the golden ones and twisted. The pear came free with a soft pop, settling into the cloth pouch.

“I did it!” she said.

“You did,” said Jun. “Now let me.”

He chose another pear and tried to twist too quickly. The basket wobbled. Tessa put a hand on the handle to steady it.

“Slowly,” she reminded him.

Jun tried again. This time the stem let go, and the second pear rolled gently into the pouch. They carried the basket to the crate and set it down. Two pears. They needed one more.

“Look,” Jun said. “That one’s perfect.”

A fat, golden pear hung near the end of a high branch. It seemed to glow between the leaves. Jun raised the basket until its ring reached the fruit.

“Easy,” Tessa said.

“I am being easy.” Jun turned the handle.

The ring caught the pear, but the stem held fast. The thin branch bent downward. A cluster of bright green buds trembled near Jun’s hand.

He turned harder.

“Stop,” Tessa said. “The branch is bending.”

Jun held still. The big pear hung just above the basket, close enough to touch if he climbed onto the crate. He looked at it, then at the crate, then at the buds quivering on the branch.

“I could reach it from there,” he said.

“Mrs. Bell said the crate wasn’t a step.”

Jun’s fingers tightened around the handle. “But Willa’s pear—”

“I know,” Tessa said. “Maybe there’s another one.”

They lowered the basket together. The pear remained on its stem, swaying lightly while the branch lifted back into place.

They searched the lower branches of the next tree. One pear was golden on one side, but when Tessa turned it, it didn’t budge.

“Not ready,” Jun said.

They left it where it was. At last, Jun found a ripe pear tucked behind a broad leaf, low enough for the basket to reach without pulling the branch. He slid the ring around it and turned carefully. The pear dropped into the pouch.

“Three!” he said.

But when they carried the basket to the crate, the third pear had a soft brown bruise on its side. It was still good to eat, but Jun frowned.

“Willa won’t want a bruised pear.”

“She might,” Tessa said. “And we can cut around it.”

Jun looked again toward the high golden pear. It still shone above them, untouched. He picked up the bruised one and turned it in his palm.

“Willa gets the best one,” he decided. “We’ll share this.”

They spread their napkin on the grass beside the crate. Tessa cut the bruise away with her little folding picnic knife. Jun broke the pear into uneven pieces, setting two on each oatcake. Tessa drizzled honey over them. The orchard smelled of warm grass and sweet fruit, and a bee wandered past their cheese before moving on.

They ate slowly, saving the neatest pear for Willa. When they returned the basket, Mrs. Bell checked the branches they had picked from. The small green buds were still there, and no leaves had been torn.

“Good gathering,” she said, looking at the pears. “That one will taste fine with the bruise cut away.”

Jun smiled. He wrapped Willa’s pear in the napkin and carried it home.

His sister was waiting with two rag dolls seated around a tiny wooden table. “You brought one!” she cried, making room beside the dolls.

Jun placed the pear between them. Through the open gate behind him, the orchard’s high pear shone in the late light, balanced on its unbent branch.
