The puppet theatre at Cedar School was no wider than a closet, but when its red curtain closed, the cardboard moon looked enormous.

At three o’clock, Nia was checking every cue on her list, Ben was trying to make the paper clouds sway, and Lila was crouched behind the little stage with Mouse on her hand. Nia wanted to finish the rehearsal before the caretaker locked the hall. Ben wanted the moon scene to look as beautiful as the scenery he had painted. Lila wanted the final scene to go perfectly without anyone noticing her.

“Mouse brings the star,” Nia read. “Then the Moon says—”

Lila reached into Mouse’s tiny pocket. Her fingers found only the lining.

“The star’s gone,” she whispered.

It was a silver felt star, no bigger than a biscuit, with a loop of blue thread at its tip. Mouse was supposed to give it to the Moon, who had been lonely in the dark. Lila had made the star herself and had carried it to school in the prop tin.

“Where did you last see it?” Ben asked.

“On the windowsill at lunch. I took it out to fix the loop.” Lila swallowed. “Then the bell rang.”

They searched the windowsill, the radiator, the prop tin, and the floor beneath the stage. Ben even lifted the cardboard clouds, though he had glued them together that morning. No star.

Nia glanced at her list. “The class is coming in five minutes.”

“We can skip the scene,” Lila said. She kept Mouse’s face turned toward the floor. “It won’t matter.”

“It’s the ending,” Ben said. “The Moon can’t be lonely forever.”

Nia shut her list. “There’s a sewing kit in Ms. Rowan’s costume cupboard. Come on.”

Ms. Rowan was sorting a box of old scarves. When Nia explained, she handed over a green sewing tin and a square of gray felt.

“Bring it back with the needle,” she said. “And don’t cut the curtain to make a star.”

“We promise,” Ben said.

Behind the stage, Nia held the felt while Ben rummaged through the tin. Lila found a brass button with a small flower stamped on it.

“We could sew this in the middle,” she said.

“It’s not silver,” Ben pointed out.

“The Moon can have a golden star,” Nia said. “It might even show better.”

Lila turned the button over in her palm. “I don’t want it to look like we forgot the real one.”

“We didn’t forget,” said Nia. “We lost it.”

That made Lila smile, just a little.

They folded the felt and cut a lopsided star. Nia threaded the needle, but the thread frayed twice. Ben held the felt tight while Lila pushed the needle through. The button was heavier than it looked. When they lifted their new star, its point sagged down.

“It’ll flop over,” Ben said.

Lila studied Mouse’s pocket. “Then it shouldn’t go in the pocket. Mouse can hold it up for the Moon to see.”

“But the Moon is meant to keep it,” Nia said.

“Mouse can tuck it into the Moon’s lap,” Lila replied. “The button will face the audience.”

She stitched a loop of thread onto the star’s top so Mouse could lift it without dropping it. Her first knot came loose. She tried again, pulling the thread tight this time.

They tested the scene. Mouse raised the star. It swung sideways, and the button flashed toward the wall.

“Wait,” Lila said. She turned the star around and tied the loop shorter. “Now.”

Mouse lifted it again. The gold button faced the theatre.

Nia nodded. “That works.”

The class filed into the little theatre, whispering and bumping knees. Ben slipped behind the cardboard scenery. Nia took her place beside the curtain with her cue list. Lila held Mouse beneath the stage, her hands trembling enough to make his ears quiver.

The rehearsal began. Mouse crossed the cardboard forest. The Moon rose above the painted hills.

Then came the final scene.

“Mouse brings the star,” Nia read.

Lila lifted Mouse. For a moment, her voice stayed trapped in her throat. She could hear the room waiting. The old silver star was gone. This one was crooked, gray, and bright with a brass button.

She could let Nia read the line instead. Or she could make the scene her own.

Lila drew a breath. “I brought you a little light,” she said—not in a whisper, but in Mouse’s clear, small voice.

The class leaned forward. Mouse offered the star. The Moon held out both felt hands, and Lila settled the star in its lap.

“I can see my way now,” Nia read as the Moon.

Ben moved the paper clouds. The curtain began to close.

Afterward, Nia returned the sewing tin to Ms. Rowan, with the needle tucked safely inside. Lila kept Mouse on her hand as the three friends stood in the empty theatre. Behind them, the red curtain met in the middle. In the dimness, the brass button on the new star caught one last dot of light.
