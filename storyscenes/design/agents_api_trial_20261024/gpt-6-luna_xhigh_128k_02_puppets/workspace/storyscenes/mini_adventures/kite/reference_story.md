# The Moon Behind the Curtain

On Thursday afternoon, Leni stayed after the last bell to paint a silver moon for the school puppet theatre. She wanted Friday’s rehearsal to run exactly on time; she had promised their teacher the class would be ready. Jo, her friend, wanted something less measurable: for Ivo, the quiet puppeteer, to enjoy the show instead of hiding behind it. “A rehearsal can be both,” Leni said, lining up her paint jars. Jo grinned. “Then let’s make room for both.”

Ivo came in carrying the rod for the moon puppet, but its felt crescent was missing. The small cloth loop that held it to the rod had torn. “I put it down for one second,” he whispered. Without the moon, the story’s boat could not find its way home. Leni checked the table and Jo searched under the chairs. The teacher’s footsteps sounded in the hall; the rehearsal was due to begin soon.

A silver edge showed beneath the stage curtain. The heavy cloth had folded over itself, and the crescent was caught in its hem. Leni started to tug. “Wait,” Jo said. He held the curtain up while Leni eased the moon free. Ivo took it carefully, as if it might be embarrassed too. The felt was whole, but the loop was split almost to its last stitch.

They needed a needle and strong thread. Mrs. Bell, the costume teacher, lent them her sewing kit. “Bring it back to the workroom when you’re done,” she said. Jo promised, and Leni opened the little case on the stage. The needle was fine, the thread long, and an old button in the kit looked cracked. “I can do the repair,” Leni said. Ivo studied the broken loop and said nothing.

Jo turned the kit over, checking its cloth lining. A brass button had slipped into the corner. “A spare,” he said, holding it out rather than immediately sewing it on. Leni turned the moon in her hands. “That could anchor the loop.” Ivo, still behind the curtain, whispered, “I don’t like when everyone can see my hands.” Jo stopped joking. “Thanks for telling us. What would make it easier?”

Ivo came a little farther into the light. “If I could hold the rod, and you didn’t grab it when it wobbles.” Leni had wanted to take over and finish quickly. She looked at the minutes left before rehearsal and at the silver felt waiting on the table. “You hold it,” she said. “I’ll sew. If it doesn’t work, we’ll find another way together.” Jo nodded, leaving the rod in Ivo’s hands.

Leni used the brass button as a new anchor and stitched the torn loop with the long thread. The extra minutes let her pass the needle through the felt twice, then secure the thread underneath. She did not hurry the final knot. Jo kept the kit open and passed her the scissors when she asked. Ivo watched the work without being asked to explain himself. When Leni finished, the moon hung from the rod again.

Jo tested it with a gentle lift. The crescent swung, but stayed attached. He tried a slightly higher arc; the stitches held. Ivo’s shoulders loosened. “May I try?” he asked. Leni handed the rod back. He moved the moon slowly across the stage, then stopped it above the painted boat. It looked like the story again—not because the repair was invisible, but because the three of them were making it together.

The curtain was still gathered at one side. Leni could have opened it all the way and taken the first cue herself, as she’d planned. Instead, she asked Ivo where he wanted the moon to appear. “Just after the boat turns,” he said. Jo set the curtain so Ivo could see his cue and the audience could see the puppet. Leni moved the boat into place. No one had to hide behind the fabric.

When the class came in for rehearsal, Ivo worked the rod. His first movement was small; his second sent the moon across the painted sea. Leni spoke the boat’s line, and Jo gave the captain a ridiculous, booming voice. Ivo laughed—an actual laugh, not the polite breath he used when people praised his puppets. The moon stayed on through the whole scene. The boat found its way home, and the rehearsal reached its ending.

Afterward, Jo returned the sewing kit to Mrs. Bell’s workroom. The brass button was now doing real work on the moon’s loop, and the scissors and needle were neatly back in their places. “It earned its trip,” he said. Mrs. Bell inspected the repair and smiled. Leni had not saved the rehearsal by doing everything herself. She had made a repair sturdy enough to trust, then let Ivo decide how to use it.

On the walk out, Ivo asked if they would rehearse the boat scene again tomorrow. “If you want,” Leni said. “I do,” he answered. Jo held the door while Ivo carried the rod. The moon was still attached, the kit was back where it belonged, and the puppeteer had chosen to return to the stage. That was enough for a good ending—and a reason to begin again.
