# The Borrowed Reel

**Prose word count, excluding heading: 951.**

At the end of the lane, the village kite workshop smelled of paper, paste, and the peppermints Mr. Vale kept in a chipped blue bowl. Mara liked all three. She had spent the morning cutting a bright orange kite, its tail stitched with scraps from old ribbons. By afternoon, she meant to fly it on the common with her friend Kit.

“Could we ask the new people, too?” Mara asked. The family in the little cottage by the pump had moved in three days ago. Their child, Ivo, watched the common from the gate but had not yet joined anybody.

Kit shrugged. “If he wants to come.”

Mara looked through the workshop window. Ivo was there, just beyond the fence, holding a folded paper bag against his chest. When she waved, he gave a small wave back and looked down.

Mr. Vale came from the storeroom carrying a wooden reel. “The wind’s up. This will do for your kite, if you bring it back before supper.”

“It isn’t ours?” Kit asked.

“Borrowed from the school club. The reel is good, but the string’s old. Mind the fray near the handle.” Mr. Vale set it carefully on the bench. “A tool is a promise to the person who lent it.”

Mara nodded. She was already picturing the kite high above the roof. Kit picked up the reel and tested its turn. It clicked once, then spun smoothly.

Outside, Ivo had moved to the workshop doorway. He watched the orange kite and the reel without stepping inside.

“Would you like to help?” Mara asked.

Ivo’s fingers tightened around the paper bag. “I don’t know how.”

“We can show you,” said Kit. “I’m Kit.”

“Ivo.” He spoke quietly, but he did not leave.

Mara wanted to hand him the reel at once. Instead she asked, “Would you rather hold the tail first?” Ivo considered this. “Maybe.” He stood by the open door, close enough to see and far enough to leave if he wanted.

Kit noticed a loose knot in the kite’s tail. “We need another scrap.” Mara opened the workshop drawer. There were ribbons in every color except orange. Ivo looked down at his bag.

“My mum wrapped my lunch with this,” he said, showing a narrow blue ribbon. “She said I could keep it.”

“You don’t have to give it away,” Mara said.

“I could lend it to the kite.” Ivo’s voice was still small, but the offer was his. Kit tied it to the tail, and Ivo held the kite while Mara tightened the knot. The blue ribbon flicked against the orange paper.

On the walk to the common, the wind strengthened. Kit carried the kite. Mara carried the reel. Ivo followed at the edge of the path, blue ribbon in hand where he had saved its loose end.

At the gate, Kit stopped. “We’ll need a runner. Ivo, you can take the kite if you like.”

Ivo shook his head. “Not yet.”

Mara felt a quick pinch of disappointment. She had imagined all three of them running together. But the afternoon was not a picture she could arrange by herself. “You can watch first,” she said. “We’ll leave room beside us.”

They reached the common. Kit held the kite up, and Mara paid out string. The orange wings caught a gust. For a heartbeat, the kite climbed. Then its tail twisted, and the old string snagged at the reel’s frayed place.

“Stop!” Mara called. She held the reel still. Kit lowered the kite before the string could snap. The kite landed in the grass, one paper corner bent. Ivo took a step back.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I made it worse with my ribbon.”

“You didn’t,” Mara said. She could see the fuzzy strand near the handle. It had been there before they left the workshop. The ribbon was still knotted neatly to the tail. The problem was the borrowed string, not Ivo’s help.

Kit examined the snag. “We can cut off the frayed part, but then there won’t be enough to fly from here.”

Mara looked at the kite, the reel, and Ivo waiting at the edge of the common. She wanted one successful flight before supper. She also knew the school club trusted Mr. Vale to return the reel in good condition. Cutting the string would solve the immediate trouble by making the borrowed tool useless.

“We should take it back and tell Mr. Vale,” she said. “We can still fly if he has another line. If he doesn’t, we’ll mend the kite and try another day.”

Kit frowned. “That means no big flight today.”

“It means we return what we borrowed,” Mara said. The answer hurt a little; it did not become easier because it was right.

Ivo came closer. “I can hold the kite while you carry the reel.”

They walked back together, the kite low between them. At the workshop, Mr. Vale listened without interrupting. He checked the damaged strand and nodded. “You did right to stop. I have a spare line, but it’s short. It’ll lift a light kite in this wind.”

Kit looked at Mara. The orange kite was light, and the blue ribbon tail fluttered gently. Mara turned to Ivo. “Would you like to help us try? You can hold the tail, or just watch.”

“I’ll hold it,” Ivo said. “Just at first.”

Mr. Vale fitted the spare line to the reel and showed them the safest way to wind it. They returned to the common, where the wind had softened. Ivo held the tail. Kit raised the frame. Mara let the line run slowly, feeling the reel turn beneath her palm.

The kite rose above the hedge, not high, but steadily. Ivo smiled at it before he remembered to hide his smile. “It’s pulling,” he said.

“Your turn to tell us when,” Mara replied.

He watched the sky, then nodded. The three of them took turns holding the line. When the kite came down, the borrowed reel was still sound, the blue ribbon was still flying, and Ivo was walking beside them instead of behind.

At the workshop, Mara returned the reel to Mr. Vale. “Thank you. We used the spare line after we told you about the fray.”

“Good,” he said. “Bring it back again when you need it.”

Ivo untied the blue ribbon and studied its small crease. “I think I’ll keep it on the kite.”

“Then it can come back next time,” Mara said.

The kite stayed in the workshop overnight, its orange wings folded and its blue tail resting on the bench. Mara had not got the grand flight she had pictured. She had got something that could happen again: a shared afternoon, a returned tool, and a new neighbour who knew where the workshop door was.
