# The Cup with the Crooked Lip

### Prose word count, excluding the title: approximately 1,000 words

Mina wanted to make her grandfather a cup before he came to the workshop on Saturday. His fingers had begun to stiffen, and the small handle on his favorite cup pinched. He never complained; he just wrapped both hands around the cup and drank slowly. Mina thought a wide handle would help. She also wanted him to know she had noticed. At the pottery workshop, Mrs. Vale was stacking bowls while the afternoon sun warmed the clay shelves. Mina had permission to use the wheel, but not the wooden rib that shaped a pot’s sides. That tool belonged to Mrs. Vale.

“Could I borrow your rib?” Mina asked. “Just for one cup. I’ll bring it back clean.” Mrs. Vale turned the smooth wooden tool in her hand. “It’s useful because it’s smooth, not because it’s magic. Keep your fingers out of the wheel, and bring it back before I start my next bowl.” Mina promised. She carried the rib to the worktable as carefully as if it were made of glass. It felt heavier than she expected, though it was only wood.

She chose a lump of clay and pressed it against the table to soften it. The sponge beside the basin was dry. Mina dunked it, squeezed it until it stopped dripping, then wiped a film of water from the wheel. “Too much water makes a good lump slide around,” Mrs. Vale said. “Too little makes it drag.” Mina nodded, wedged the clay, and centered it. The wheel hummed beneath her hands. At first the lump wobbled. She pressed steadily until it rose, round and quiet, beneath her palms.

Before she pulled the walls, Mina glanced at the old cup she had drawn in her notebook. Grandpa’s handle was broad, with a small thumb hollow. She remembered the way he used to turn it until his thumb found that comfortable dent. Mina pinched a wide handle onto her cup and added a shallow thumb rest. It was not fancy. It was the sort of detail he might feel before he noticed it.

The wheel began to knock. Someone had nudged its foot pedal, and the wheel’s slow turn was no longer even. Mina tried to correct the rim with her fingers, but one side climbed higher than the other. She reached for Mrs. Vale’s rib and drew it along the wall. The clay smoothed under the wood, yet the rim stayed crooked. The more Mina tried to force it straight, the thinner one side became.

Omi, a younger apprentice, peered over the table. “Is it meant to lean?”

“It wasn’t,” Mina said. She wished he had not seen. Omi held up his own clay lump. “My sponge is a puddle. Can I borrow yours?” Mina slid her cup away from the open window, onto a sheltered shelf, then passed over the sponge. Omi wrung it out with both hands. “Mrs. Vale says rushing the drying makes a thin wall crack.” Mina watched the cup rest safely out of the draft while Omi fixed his sticky clay.

When he returned the sponge, Mina studied the rim. The crooked side was real. She could use the leftover clay to add a little to it and rub the line smooth. That might make the cup look more even, but she would have to press hard, and the wall was already thin. Or she could leave the uneven edge and shape its lowest point into a small pouring lip. Grandpa would see that it was not perfect. He might even notice the part she had tried to hide.

Mrs. Vale glanced over, but did not take the choice away. “What do you want the cup to do?” she asked. Mina imagined Grandpa lifting it with stiff fingers, then pouring the last of his tea. “Be easy to hold,” Mina said. “And not pretend I made it perfectly.” She pinched a small, useful notch at the low edge and smoothed the rest with the rib. The line still wandered. Now it looked deliberate, though not quite straight.

Mina washed the rib and returned it to its peg before Mrs. Vale began her next bowl. She checked it with her thumb; there was no clay left on its smooth edge. Mrs. Vale took it down and used it to shape the bowl. “Still useful,” she said. Mina felt relieved. She had borrowed it for a job, not to keep it as a trophy.

She brushed clear glaze over the cup and carried it to the kiln shelf. Omi’s sheltered spot had helped it dry slowly. The kiln fired steadily. When the cup cooled, its glaze shone softly, and the crooked lip had not cracked. Mina carried it to Grandpa’s kitchen herself. He looked at the wide handle first, then the uneven rim. “A little crooked,” he said.

“I tried to straighten it,” Mina admitted. “Then I thought this side could help you pour.” Grandpa wrapped his fingers around the handle. His thumb settled into the hollow. He poured tea from the low lip into his saucer, then lifted the cup. Nothing dripped onto the table. “That works better than my old one,” he said. He drank, smiling over the rim. Mina watched him hold it comfortably. The cup was not straight, and it did its job. She decided that was a better kind of finished.
