# The Two Pegs

<!-- Prose word count, heading excluded: approximately 1,000 words. -->

At the local museum, Mara was supposed to help with the new shadow exhibit before Saturday’s visitors arrived. She wanted her name on the best display in the activity room; her cousin Tavi wanted to know why the giant silhouette on the poster never matched the little one on the screen. The attendant, Mr. Bell, lent them a stout torch and pointed out its return tray. “Borrowed means it comes back,” he said. “Useful answers come back with it.” Mara smiled as though this were already a promise.

The exhibit had a paper figure, a white screen, and two pegs marked on the table. A printed label promised a giant shadow on the screen-side peg. The figure had been left there, its thin legs nearly touching the wall. “Maybe the picture is just showing the best angle,” Mara said. Tavi held the torch steady while she switched it on. The beam was bright, but the figure made only a small, ordinary outline.

Mara tried lifting the figure, turning it, and moving the torch all at once. Each attempt changed something, but none made the poster appear. Tavi stopped her before she could tug the screen loose. “If we change everything, we won’t know what helped.” Mara disliked waiting while an answer hid in a simple question. Still, she set the figure back down. Their first demonstration had made her puzzled, not proud.

Tavi read the small print aloud. It said to place the figure on the screen-side peg for a giant shadow. “It’s already there,” Mara said. “That’s not the giant one.” She wanted to cross out the instruction, but Tavi put his pencil down. “First we should see what the other peg does.” The confident words on the label made the small shadow feel less like a mistake and more like a mystery they had to earn their way through.

A younger visitor named June had been waiting near the next table. The little shadow startled her, and she called it a ghost. Mara showed her the paper figure and let June move her hand between the torch and the screen. The ghost changed shape; it did not chase anyone. June laughed and stayed to watch. “Show me when you solve it,” she said. Mara promised a real answer, not a spooky story.

Tavi sketched the first outline in his notebook, exactly as it looked. Mara wanted to draw a larger one beside it, to make the display more impressive. “Then we’d be making the result up,” Tavi said. She sighed, but let him mark the peg and screen distance. The unflattering little drawing turned into a useful record. If the next attempt changed, they would know they had not simply remembered the first one incorrectly.

They switched the torch off. Tavi moved the paper figure to the lamp-side peg, farther from the screen and nearer the beam. Mara measured the distance with the paper ruler supplied for the exhibit. Because sunlight washed over the screen, she drew the room shade across the high window. The same torch, figure, and screen remained in place. “Only the distance changed,” Tavi said. “Now we can blame the right thing.”

When Mara switched the torch on, the new shadow spread wide across the screen. It was crisp enough to see the figure’s crooked crown. Tavi measured again; the wall had not moved, and the paper figure had not grown. June watched from her chair, now asking whether she could make a dragon next. Mara compared the two outlines in the notebook. The nearer the figure was to the torch, the larger its shadow became. The label had the pegs reversed.

Mara wanted to rub the whole card off the wall. Tavi stopped her again, this time gently. “If we erase it, people won’t know what was wrong.” They turned the card over and wrote a correction: lamp-side peg for the giant shadow. Mara placed the figure there and made the large outline once more. It felt better to repair the exhibit than to win an argument with a sheet of paper.

June named the shadow a dragon and asked how to make its wings wider. Mara showed her that moving the figure closer to the torch changed the size, while turning the paper changed its shape. Tavi let June hold the ruler and find the lamp-side mark herself. Their demonstration was not the poster’s impossible picture; it was a repeatable experiment a visitor could try. Mara’s name went on the small card as the demonstrator, and Tavi’s measurements went beside it.

Before leaving, they switched off the torch and returned it to Mr. Bell. He read the correction, then used their sketch to check that the comparison was fair. “This will save the next group a lot of guessing,” he said. Mara had wanted the biggest display; she was pleased that the useful part was something she and Tavi had made together. June waved from the screen as they left, already practicing a dragon with her fingers.
