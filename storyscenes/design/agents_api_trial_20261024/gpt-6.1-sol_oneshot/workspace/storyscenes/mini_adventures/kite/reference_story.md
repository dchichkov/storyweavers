# The Space Beside the Reel

Mara had made a yellow kite at the village workshop, and she wanted somebody to see it fly. Not just anybody. Through the open window she could see the new neighbour sitting on his front step, turning a small wooden wheel between his fingers. His name was Ivo. Yesterday Mara had said hello so loudly that he had dropped the wheel and gone indoors.

Today she would do better. She pressed a blue paper patch over one corner of her kite. The patch looked like a little window. Perhaps, from the field, Ivo would be able to see the blue against the yellow. Perhaps he would ask whether he could have a turn.

“You'll need string before admiration,” said Nan Bell, who ran the workshop. She was sorting bamboo sticks into a biscuit tin. Mara searched beneath the scraps on her bench. Her own string was still wound round the chair at home, where her little brother had been building a railway. She had forgotten it completely.

Nan took a red reel from the shelf. “Borrow mine. Bring it back before I close.” She rolled it across the bench, keeping one finger on the loose end. Mara caught it with both hands. The reel was heavier than it looked, with smooth wooden cheeks and a little dent shaped like a thumbnail.

A fuzzy place in the string caught Mara's eye. She pulled it straight, then stopped. Several fibres had lifted away from the twist. Nan held out her scissors. “Better a shorter sound line than a long doubtful one.” Outside, children were already rattling the gate on their way to the field.

Mara could have hidden the fuzzy place beneath the next turn. Instead she cut beyond it and tied a fresh loop. Nan tested the knot with a firm tug. They wound the loose string neatly between them. Mara had lost some length and several minutes, but the reel now turned without catching under her thumb.

At Ivo's step she nearly called, “Come flying!” Instead she sat on the low wall, leaving room between them. “I've got a kite with a window,” she said. Ivo looked at the yellow paper. “Does anything live inside?” Mara turned it over. “Only glue.” He smiled without looking directly at her.

She asked about his wheel. It belonged to a broken toy cart, he said. He liked how it spun even without the cart. Mara offered the reel for comparison, keeping hold of the string end. He turned it once, slowly. “This one's got a job,” he said. She thought that was a good description.

From the workshop doorway came a little pattering sound. A sparrow was hopping through the fallen bamboo shavings. Mara stood still instead of chasing it away. Ivo pointed to a shaving caught on its foot. The bird shook itself free and flew to the gutter, leaving the shaving turning gently on the stones.

“Would you hold the reel?” Mara asked. “You don't have to shout anything.” Ivo looked towards the field, where a red kite was already bobbing over the hedge. “Not in the middle.” Mara wanted the middle. That was where everyone would notice her yellow kite. She swallowed the answer she had been about to give.

They chose the lower edge of the field, beyond the noisy gate but clear of the hedge. Mara laid the kite face down while Ivo held the reel. There was no crowd around them. There was only the sound of paper moving against grass, and someone laughing far away near the football posts.

Mara lifted the kite and ran. It rose to the height of her head, tipped sideways, and landed on its blue corner. Ivo stopped the reel before string spilled over the rim. Mara stared at the grass stain. She had imagined a clean, easy launch, with Ivo looking pleased and everyone else looking impressed.

“It's leaning,” Ivo said. Mara almost told him that kites always leaned before they flew. Then she saw him pointing, not at the sky, but at the tail. One ribbon had caught around a stick. She freed it and spread the ribbons apart. Ivo kept the line loose while she worked.

The second time she ran more slowly. A small steady wind came up the slope. The yellow paper pulled against her fingers, and she let go. Ivo allowed the reel to turn. The kite climbed above the hedge, its blue patch briefly dark against the afternoon sky. Mara forgot to look towards the other children.

“It's heavy now,” Ivo said. Mara came beside him and put her hands beneath his, not over them. Together they felt the tug. When he moved one thumb, the reel turned a fraction. The kite rose another little distance. “That's you doing that,” Mara said. Ivo watched the yellow shape without answering.

They did not fly highest. Once the red kite passed theirs, and Mara felt a small hot wish to let out every remaining turn. But the sound line was shorter now. She left the final wraps on the reel. Ivo was still holding it, and she did not want his first good tug to end with an empty wooden wheel.

When the workshop bell rang, they brought the kite down together. Mara held the frame while Ivo wound in the string, moving it from side to side so that it lay evenly. A grass seed had caught in the loop. He picked it out and handed it to her as if it were something valuable.

Nan took the reel and tried it between her palms. It turned freely. Mara showed her where they had shortened the line. “It flew far enough,” she said. Nan set it on its shelf, ready for the morning class. Ivo laid the yellow kite beside Mara's bag, carefully keeping the stained corner off the wet doorstep.

Outside, he stopped beside his own gate. “Can I make a kite with a door?” he asked. Mara began to say they could make the biggest kite in the village. Then she thought of the space they had left beside the reel. “Yes,” she said. “You can choose where it goes.” He opened the gate and stayed to talk.
