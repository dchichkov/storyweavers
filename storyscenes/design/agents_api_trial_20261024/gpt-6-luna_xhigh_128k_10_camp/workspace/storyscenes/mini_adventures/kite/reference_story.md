# The Last Bright Bar

At four o’clock, the family campsite smelled of pine needles, toast, and the damp towels Mom had hung between two trees. Mira was old enough to help with the tent and young enough to want Dad to notice. She had tightened every peg that morning. Kit, her little brother, had made a blanket cave beside the bedrolls and put his stuffed fox, Pip, in charge.

Mira hoped Dad would let her lead the short walk to the washroom after supper. He usually said she hurried when Kit was with her. Kit hoped nobody would move the bedrolls before he found Pip. They wanted different things, but for now their small campsite felt like a whole country they had made together.

A gust lifted the blanket cave. Kit crawled out to rescue Pip, then helped Mira carry a cup to the picnic table. He was sure he had tucked the fox under the folded rainfly by the tent. He did not notice the soft toy slipping from his jacket pocket when he bent to pick up a pinecone.

By the time the sun touched the ridge, Kit had checked the sleeping bags twice. He looked beneath the table, then under his own knees. “Pip’s gone,” he said. His voice had gone thin. The trees beyond the tent had grown long, dark shadows, and Kit kept glancing at them as if something there might have borrowed the fox.

Mira wanted to say it was only a toy. Instead she looked at his empty hands. “We’ll find him before supper.” Kit pressed his lips together. “You said that about my blue sock.” “That sock was in your boot.” “It was a very sneaky sock.” He almost smiled, but his eyes still watched the tree line.

Dad was stirring a pot at the fire. Mira asked to borrow the battery lantern from the picnic table. “The tent’s bright enough,” he said, “but the path will be dim soon. Take it, stay together, and bring it back before I serve supper.” Mira promised. The lantern was heavier than she expected, warm from the afternoon sun.

She switched it on. Its circle of light spread over Kit’s shoes and the tent wall. Kit stepped behind her. “The trees are bigger when it gets late,” he murmured. Mira felt a prickly wish to prove she was not scared either. She could have marched off alone and called it brave. Instead, she waited until he stood beside her.

On the tent wall, their shadows stretched up like giants. Mira made two ears with her fingers. “That’s Pip,” Kit said. His shadow gave the fox a tail, then made it bow. Kit laughed, surprising himself. When Mira offered him the lantern, he took it with both hands. “I can carry the sun,” he said, though he kept close to her sleeve.

Kit remembered placing Pip under the rainfly, but something bothered him. He had worn his jacket while helping Mira gather pinecones near the fern hollow. He patted the pocket that had carried the toy. “Maybe he fell out there,” he said. “Or maybe I just want him to be there.” Mira could hear the uncertainty in his voice. She let him finish.

Dad came over with a handful of orange reflector tabs. He clipped them along the guyline that ran from the tent toward the fern hollow. “These catch the lantern light,” he said. “If you lose sight of camp, follow them back. I’ll keep the fire going.” He did not offer to search for them. He knew Kit needed to try, and Mira needed to lead without pretending she knew everything.

Mira counted two bright bars on the lantern’s battery display. One careful sweep at Kit’s remembered spot would leave one bar. If Pip was not there, they could turn back, or spend the last bar searching the fern hollow and return by the reflectors. The second way might work. It also meant trusting the path once the lantern went dark.

They walked to the tent edge first. Mira swept the beam beneath the rainfly and across the bedrolls. Kit lifted a corner of canvas. There was a pinecone, a mitten, and a crumb of toast, but no fox. Kit’s shoulders folded in. “I did put him here,” he said. “I believe you,” Mira answered. “I just don’t think he stayed.”

The lantern showed one bar. Mira could turn around now and keep the light for the walk back. She could also use it to search the fern hollow, where Kit’s jacket had brushed the branches. “If we go farther, we’ll come back by Dad’s reflectors,” she said. “No guessing once the light is gone.” Kit looked down the path. “I want to look. I can help.”

Mira handed him the lantern. It was his turn to choose where the beam landed. Kit walked slowly past the guyline tabs, and Mira held his free hand where the wet roots crossed the path. At the fern hollow he searched the branches while Mira checked the ground. The lantern lit a corner of bright cloth beneath a curled leaf.

“Pip!” Kit dropped to his knees. The fox was caught beneath a flap of rainfly that had blown loose from the tent, its stitched smile muddy but familiar. Kit pulled him free and hugged him against his chest. Mira’s knees were damp. Neither of them cared. The lantern’s final bar blinked once, then faded.

For a moment the woods seemed very large again. Then Kit spotted a reflector wink ahead. They followed the orange points, hand in hand, until the firelight found their faces. Kit was still nervous, but he was no longer hiding behind Mira. He had carried the light, and he had helped find the way back.

Dad took the lantern from Mira. “Pip’s back,” she said. “And the lantern got us home.” Dad nodded and set it beside the cooking pot; it had done its important work. Kit wiped the fox with a clean corner of his blanket. “He’s muddy,” he said, “but so are you.” Mira looked at her knees. “Then we match.”

At supper, Kit placed Pip on the empty chair between them. Mira told Dad where they had searched and why they had turned back by the reflectors. Dad listened without interrupting. When he asked who had led the way, Kit raised the lantern’s dark handle. “We did,” he said. Mira liked that answer better than being called brave by herself.

Later, Kit tucked Pip beside his pillow and asked Mira to leave the tent flap open a finger’s width. She did. Their shadows on the canvas were small again, moving whenever the fire shifted. Kit watched them without shrinking. Mira had wanted to prove she could lead; she had learned that leading meant leaving room for someone else to carry the light.
