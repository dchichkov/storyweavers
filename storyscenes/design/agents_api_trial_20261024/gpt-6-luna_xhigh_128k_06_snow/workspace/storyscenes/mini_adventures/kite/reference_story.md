# The Rope in the Snow

The village square had disappeared under fresh snow, except for the dark stone around the fountain. Mina stamped her boots and read the shopping list again: bread, soup, and lamp oil for Mr. Vale, whose bad knee made the walk to the market difficult. She wanted to prove she could manage the errand without her mother walking beside her. The basket handles already cut into her fingers. Across the square, Jo skimmed past on a borrowed sled, laughing whenever the runners whispered over packed snow.

At Dima’s lending stall, Mina asked to borrow the little wooden sled. Dima tested its rope, then pressed a boot against one runner. It shifted a little. “It’ll carry a careful load,” he said. “Bring it back ready for the next person.” Mina promised. Dima set the wrapped provisions beside her. She tied the basket down, then checked the knot twice. The soup flask sat upright between the bread and the oil bottle. “That is quite a cargo,” Jo said, arriving breathless. “A race to Mr. Vale’s door?”

Mina looked at the snow-packed road. Jo had been asking for a race all morning, ever since the first flakes made the square into a bright, smooth track. “Not with soup aboard,” Mina said. “We can race after.” Jo’s face pinched. “You always say later when you mean no.” Mina felt the old urge to agree just to keep Jo smiling. But Mr. Vale had asked for the lamp oil before dusk, and a spilled flask would be no good to anyone.

A baker rang a little brass bell beside his doorway. He had finished sweeping the narrow lane behind the bakery, the one that curved toward Mr. Vale’s cottage. Jo pointed it out. “That’s clearer than the main road.” Mina tested the sled rope again. The mittens made her fingers clumsy, but they kept the cold from biting through. Jo kicked at a ridge of snow. “We could still get there first.” Mina answered, “We could get there with the soup, too.”

They started along the swept lane. The sled bumped over one buried stone, and the loose runner rattled. Mina stopped, eased the basket toward the middle, and retied the rope around the handle. Jo watched the square and its racers disappear behind the bakery. “I hate being the one who waits,” Jo said. Mina’s cheeks warmed, though the air stung. “I’m not asking you to wait. I’m asking you to help me get this there.” Jo did not answer, but took the other side of the rope.

Beyond the bakery, the lane climbed. The runner scraped once more; Mina slowed until the sled tracked straight. She could hear children calling near the fountain, and Jo kept glancing back. Mina wondered if being responsible meant becoming dull, or if that was just what it felt like when she was afraid to disappoint someone. Then the basket shifted. Jo caught it before the flask tipped. The two of them steadied the load together. “Good catch,” Mina said. “I was watching the hill,” Jo replied. “Someone had to.”

At the cottage, Mr. Vale opened the door with his scarf still around his shoulders. Mina brought the basket inside while Jo held the sled steady on the step. Mr. Vale smiled at the bread and soup, then took the lamp oil to his little table. “You made it before the light went,” he said. Mina had worried that he would ask why they were late. Instead, he poured them each a mug of warm apple drink. Jo stopped looking toward the square and listened while Mr. Vale described the snow on the hillside.

They took the empty sled back together. At Dima’s stall, the runner had loosened farther on the trip. Mina told him before he had to ask. Dima set the sled on its side and tightened the metal fitting with a small wrench. Jo held the handle steady. “It did its job,” he said. Dima nodded. “And now it’s ready for the next one.” Mina returned the rope and thanked him. The sled had carried the things Mr. Vale needed; returning it in working order felt like part of the errand, not an extra favor.

Jo walked Mina to the edge of the square, where the other children had already gone inside. “I’m sorry I kept pushing,” Jo said. “I wanted the race more than I wanted to help.” Mina was glad Jo had admitted it, and gladder still that Jo had stayed. “You helped when the basket shifted,” she said. “That mattered.” They shared the last heel of bread Dima had tucked into the basket. The fountain was almost buried, the snow kept falling, and Mina went home knowing she had kept her promise without having to do everything alone.
