# The Book Between Returns

<!-- About 1,000 prose words; the heading is excluded. -->

At 5:24 on Thursday, Lio was choosing a book for the library’s reading-club table when Nia hurried in from the children’s corner. She was seven, small enough that the atlas shelves seemed built for giants, and she was trying very hard not to cry. The Moon in My Pocket, her favorite picture book, had vanished. She had brought it in to return, then couldn’t remember whether she had left it by the window seat or the returns desk. The library closed at six. Lio wanted to collect the new comic he had reserved before the desk shut, but Nia looked at him as if he might know how to make the shelves behave.

“I’ll find it before closing,” he said. He meant it. Nia’s mother was still talking with someone near the entrance, and Nia didn’t want to go home without the book. Lio asked Ms Vale, the librarian, whether he could borrow the book trolley. Ms Vale agreed, on the condition that he pushed it slowly and kept it out of the doorway. Lio helped move a small stack of returned books onto it. The trolley cleared the aisle and gave them somewhere to sort their search. Its left wheel squeaked whenever he turned too sharply.

Nia remembered a silver moon on the cover, but not where she had put the book. Lio checked the library catalogue. The screen said the title belonged on the low shelf beside the animal stories. “Good,” he said. “That’s easy.” Nia glanced at the date on the screen. “It says Tuesday.” The catalogue had not been updated since the last reshelving. Lio could have ignored her and started searching the shelf. Instead, he wrote down the title and the old location, so they could tell the difference between a useful clue and a guess.

They started with the returns trolley. Lio lifted each book carefully instead of rattling the stack. Nia read the titles aloud, sometimes stopping to admire a ridiculous drawing. The missing book wasn’t there. By the low shelves, they found a book about a badger wearing slippers, and Nia put it in the correct slot. Still no silver moon. The trolley squeaked again when Lio pulled it around the end of the aisle. “It’s announcing our expedition,” he said. “Then tell it to whisper,” Nia replied, and for a moment they both laughed.

At the window seat, Nia looked beneath a cushion. “I was reading there,” she said, “but I remember carrying it away.” She had a paper bookmark in her pocket, folded from a scrap of blue paper, with a silver moon she had drawn herself. She showed it to Lio, then let him hold it while she checked the cushion seam. The bookmark didn’t reveal where the book had gone, but it made the title feel less like a record on a screen and more like something Nia had made her own.

A younger child asked why they were looking under chairs. Nia answered before Lio could: “My book got lost, and we’re checking the places it might be.” The child pointed toward a picture-book display, then went back to a grown-up. Lio noticed that Nia had stopped waiting for him to explain everything. She took the list and marked the places they had checked. “You can push,” she said. “I can remember.” Lio gave her the pencil. It was a small change, but the search began to feel like theirs instead of his promise dragging her behind it.

Ms Vale came back from the adult stacks and listened to their list. She checked the reshelving note. The low-shelf entry was old; a book with the same title had been moved to the top shelf during a rearrangement. Ms Vale didn’t let Lio climb alone. She inspected the step ladder, set its feet on the level floor, and held it while he reached only as far as she directed. Nia stayed beside the trolley and watched the ladder, not the clock. Lio’s reserved comic would be removed from the hold shelf at six. If they spent too long here, he would miss it.

“Maybe we should go get your comic,” Nia said. She wasn’t asking him to break his promise; she was trying to make the disappointment smaller. Lio pictured the comic waiting with his name on it. He could hurry to the desk and hope Nia’s book turned up later. Or he could finish the search carefully, even if his own book went back onto the shelf. “We came for yours first,” he said. “I’m not climbing unless Ms Vale says it’s safe.” Ms Vale nodded. “That’s the right order.” The choice cost him something ordinary and real: the book he had been excited to take home would not be his that evening.

Behind a row of oversized atlases, Lio saw the silver moon. The Moon in My Pocket had been slipped onto the top shelf instead of the picture-book shelves. Ms Vale steadied the ladder as he reached it. Nia recognized the crease on the cover and held out her bookmark. “I knew it was in here somewhere,” she said, though she had not known where. Lio returned the bookmark between the first pages. Nia held the book against her chest, no longer near tears.

They brought the trolley back to its bay. Lio put the rest of the returns where Ms Vale could sort them, and Nia carried her book to the desk. His reserved comic had already gone back into the collection. He felt the loss of it for a minute; then Nia asked if he wanted to hear the page she liked best. Outside, the closing bell sounded. Lio had kept his promise, and the trolley had done more than carry books: it had made room for Nia to help. She opened the cover, and the silver moon bookmark waited where they had put it.
