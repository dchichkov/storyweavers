# The Window Between Them

Mara carried the waxed paper parcel under her coat, although the rain had stopped. At the allotment gate she took it out and rubbed a thumb across the string. Inside were runner beans from the shop on Bell Street. They were not the beans Ivo's grandfather had saved, but they were the same red-flowered sort. She had asked twice.

Ivo was inside the greenhouse, lifting dead stems into a bucket. Through the misted glass he looked like somebody working underwater. Mara had promised to water his beans while he visited his grandfather. She had gone once, then forgotten. When he came home she said the tap must have failed. Neither of them had mentioned the tap since.

The gate caught on a stone. Usually she would have called for him to lift it from the other side. Today she crouched, pushed the stone into the hedge and eased the gate open herself. Ivo looked up at the sound. He did not wave, but he did not turn his back either.

By the greenhouse door stood their old measuring stick. Last summer they had put a pencil line on it every Sunday, waiting for the beans to grow taller than they were. The highest line had Ivo's initials beside it because Mara had been away that week. She touched the mark and then took her hand away. It was not hers to rub out.

"Can I come in?" she asked.

"Don't catch your coat on the latch. It's loose."

She held the door until he had put down the bucket, then stepped inside. Warm, damp air closed around her. The empty bean pots were still in their row, their sticks leaning together at the top like a tent nobody wanted to sleep in. She laid the parcel on the dry end of the bench.

"These are for you."

Ivo did not undo the string. "Did the shop have a tap too?"

She had rehearsed a sentence about being busy. Now it sounded like something belonging to a stranger. "The tap wasn't broken. I forgot. Then I lied because I wanted you to let me come back."

He picked a crumb of soil off the bench. "You could have told me before I spent an hour taking it apart."

"I know."

"No. You know now."

A snail was travelling along the rim of an empty pot. Mara almost pointed it out. They used to give snails grand names and carry them to the compost heap. Instead she waited while Ivo took it outside on a leaf. When he came back, he left the door open between them and the path.

The first gust made the door bang against its stop. A second lifted the loose corner of the roof vent. Rain shook off the glass in a sheet, and the vent stayed open. Directly below it stood a tray of Ivo's young tomatoes, each stem no thicker than a match. The wind bent them all the same way.

Ivo reached for the vent handle. Its cord came away in his hand.

"Hold the door," he said.

Mara held it. He fetched the wooden prop from beneath the bench, but it was too short to reach the vent. For a moment they both stared at the gap. Above the allotment hedge, another dark cloud was coming towards them.

There was a roll of repair tape on the shelf. Mara lifted it down. The cardboard centre showed through the last ragged turn. She pulled; a strip shorter than her finger came away. She put it where Ivo could see it instead of saying it would probably do.

"We could move the tomatoes," she said.

"The cold frame's full. And they'll snap if we carry the tray into this."

She looked at the parcel. The paper was thick and greasy, with broad folded corners. Spread flat, it would cover the gap at the bottom of the vent. The beans inside would have to go somewhere else. There was water on most of the bench now.

"I've got paper," she said. "But I need something dry for the beans."

Ivo passed her his empty sandwich tin. It had one stubborn hinge. She waited while he opened it, rather than pulling it out of his hands. Then she untied the parcel and tipped the beans into the tin. One hit the bench; she caught it before it rolled into a puddle.

They climbed no higher than the broad bottom shelf. Ivo held the vent down while Mara tucked the waxed paper under its edge. The short strip of tape held one corner. For the other they used the parcel string, tying it around the handle and the iron shelf bracket. When they stepped back, the paper swelled, settled and held.

The tomatoes stopped trembling. Under the paper patch the greenhouse light was yellow instead of grey. Mara shut the door and fastened the low bolt. Ivo stood beside her without speaking until the next gust had passed.

"Those beans still aren't Grandad's," he said.

"No."

"And this doesn't fix last week."

"No."

He opened the sandwich tin. On the inside of its lid was a little square of paper listing his grandfather's planting dates. He studied it, then moved half the empty pots towards her. "We can start these. Together. I'm not leaving them with you yet."

Mara filled a pot and pressed the soil down too hard. Ivo showed her with two fingers how lightly he meant. She emptied it and started again. They sowed six beans each, making a hollow with the end of a pencil. There were more beans in the tin, but neither hurried to finish them.

At the gate, Ivo gave her the measuring stick to carry back inside. She stood it beside the new pots without adding a line. Above them the parcel paper made a small breathing sound whenever the wind passed. Through its amber square of light, the twelve wet circles of soil looked almost warm.
