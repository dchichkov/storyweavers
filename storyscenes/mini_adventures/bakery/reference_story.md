# The Light Under the Cloth

Nell had hidden the paper crown inside the flour bin, which was an excellent place until someone wanted flour. Now it sat on her head, white along one edge, while she swept the bakery floor. Across the lane, Mr Vale was closing his bicycle workshop for the last time. Tomorrow his daughter would take him to live beside the sea. Tonight the village meant to give him supper.

"He mustn't find out," Nell told her brother Kit.

"Take the crown off, then."

She put it under the counter. The great plaited loaf was still rising in its tin. Everyone had made one strand: even Mr Vale's daughter, who had stayed long enough to press her thumb into the dough before catching her train. Nell had promised to carry it into the shop when the clock struck seven.

The oven room had no window. Its electric bulb had failed that morning, and the baker had gone to fetch a new one from town. Nell could see the oven door from the passage, but not the little thermometer beside it. She would need Mr Vale's lantern. She rehearsed asking for it without mentioning a loaf, a supper, a crown, or seven o'clock.

Mr Vale was tying string around a bundle of spanners when she arrived. "You look as if you've swallowed a question."

"Could we borrow your lantern? The bakery light's gone."

"Certainly. Bring it back before I lock up. I need it for the cellar steps."

He passed it to her with its handle folded flat. Nell lifted the handle and held the lantern well away from her skirt. It had a little dent shaped like a thumbnail. She remembered making that dent years ago, when she had knocked it off his bench. He had said a dent was better than a child with a burned hand.

Outside, Kit pointed to the workshop clock. "We could just tell him."

"And have him say thank you before we've done anything? No."

They carried the lantern back across the lane. Kit held the bakery door while Nell brought it inside. A customer had left a green wool glove beside the till. Kit fitted it over his own hand; its fingers hung empty beyond his fingertips. Nell laughed despite herself, then pinned it to the noticeboard where its owner would see it in the morning.

In the oven room the lantern made the thermometer shine. Nell set it on the stone shelf, clear of the towels. Kit lifted the loaf tin with both hands. The dough trembled like a sleeping creature. Nell opened the oven, and together they slid the tin onto the rack.

"That's everybody's bread in there," Kit said.

"Then don't slam the door."

He closed it as carefully as a bedroom door. Through the wall came the workshop bell: one clear note, then another. Mr Vale rang it whenever he wanted someone to mind his counter. Nell went to the passage and listened. No third note followed. Perhaps he had only been packing it.

While the loaf baked, they folded the tablecloth. One corner was darned with blue thread. Nell's mother always put that corner at the wall, but Kit laid it toward the empty chair at the head of the table.

"He fixes things," he said. "He won't mind a mend."

Nell left it there. She wrote VALE on the crown, then turned it over and wrote THANK YOU instead. His name was on every bill and parcel he had packed today. This should say something different.

A knock came at the back door. Nell hid the crown against her chest. Mr Vale stood on the step, holding his cellar key.

"My daughter called. The van's coming early. Could I have the lantern now?"

Behind Nell the oven gave a small metallic tick. The loaf needed another ten minutes. Without the lantern she could not read the thermometer or see the crust. She could return it and wait for the baker, but the supper would begin without its centerpiece. She could ask Mr Vale to wait without explaining. His bags were already stacked by the workshop door.

"There's something in the oven," she said.

"I rather hoped there would be."

"It's for you."

She showed him the crown. Flour fell from its fold onto her shoes. For a moment he only looked at the uneven letters. Then he took his cap off, as though she had offered him something too hot to hold.

"I can carry a lantern down two steps later," he said. "Would you like someone to look at your loaf?"

Nell let him into the oven room. He did not take over. He held the light while Kit opened the door, and Nell tapped the loaf with her knuckles. A hollow sound answered. They lifted it out together and left it on the rack. Where the strands crossed, one had swollen over another. Nell thought she could tell whose was whose, but she couldn't be sure anymore.

By the time the others arrived, Mr Vale had sliced the first heel. There was no sudden shout of surprise. People stopped in the doorway, then came in talking, and someone fetched another chair for Kit. Nell set the lantern beside Mr Vale's plate, its flame extinguished now that the baker had returned with the bulb.

"I'm sorry you found out," she said.

Mr Vale broke the heel in two and gave her half. "I was going to eat standing up tonight."

The blue darn lay beside his wrist. He put on the paper crown without brushing off the flour. In the dark workshop across the lane, all the tools were packed; here, his lantern stood among the crumbs, with enough room around it for everyone's hands.
