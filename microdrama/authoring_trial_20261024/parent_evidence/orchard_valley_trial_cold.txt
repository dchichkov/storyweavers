The White Ribbons

Mara had tied white ribbons to the six orchard rows that morning. Each ribbon marked fruit promised to a different kitchen, including the kitchen where Ivo cooked for the school. Now the weather bell was ringing for an overnight frost. There were blankets in the shed, but the branches were too low to drape carelessly. “The bees ruined the early blossoms,” Mara said, repeating the explanation she had heard all week. “Perhaps covering these will keep them out.” Ivo looked at the darkening sky. They had a few hours, and no spare spring.

At the end of the first row, Mara bent over the damaged branch they had kept for comparison. The unopened buds were black inside; even flowers under a fine mesh bag had the same injury. A dated frost marker beside the branch matched the night of the damage. Bees had never entered that bag. “Cold,” she said at last. “We have been blaming the thing that brings us fruit.” The discovery did not mend a single blossom. It did change what she meant to shut out tonight.

Ivo brought out his father's field notebook, its swollen leather cover held shut with string. On the orchard bench, Mara opened a page showing cloth raised clear of the flowers and edges anchored against the ground. The words and drawing belonged together: a pocket of still air, not a wet blanket pressing on petals. She traced the drawing with a dirty finger, then said both instructions aloud. Ivo waited while she checked the page once more. This time she had attended to the whole method.

Mara raised the cloth clear of the comparison branch and anchored every edge. But the cold had already sharpened beyond what this thin roof could resist. The covered cup chilled despite the sound construction. She checked the gap and stones again; nothing had slipped. “We built it properly,” she said, “but it is not enough for this cold.” The failed trial left her without a working shelter to repeat before darkness. Knowledge had put a roof in her hands; it could not make the cloth thicker.

Ivo held out his long fork, the one with his initials burned into the handle. It could lift cloth above the branches without tearing it. “Borrow it until morning,” he said. Mara took it carefully; the tool was still his, even while her hands controlled it. Then he rolled up his sleeves. “You raise the roof; I'll carry stones and close the sides.” They would be able to move along the rows together, with neither leaving a half-finished shelter behind.

Mara carried the fork between the rows, then stopped. The trial roof had stood properly, but the cold had beaten its thin cloth. Making six more roofs from the same material would not give them greater resistance. She folded the blankets beside the gate while Ivo checked the empty supports. They had a sound method and too little insulation, with no time to fetch heavier covers. Six white ribbons moved above the exposed rows as frost silvered the latch.

At sunrise Mara returned to the ribbons. Every exposed row held black-centered flowers. No living blossoms remained among the six marked rows. She counted carefully: no rows had surviving blossoms. There was no quarrel left to have with the bees. Cold had done this, and the night's work had determined how much of it the orchard could withstand.

Mara wiped the fork's handle and returned it to Ivo. His initials emerged from beneath the soil. She gathered all six white ribbons and laid them beside the shut notebook. Beyond the bench, a bee landed on an unmarked weed in bloom. Mara left it undisturbed.
