The White Ribbons

Mara had tied white ribbons to the six orchard rows that morning. Each ribbon marked fruit promised to a different kitchen, including the kitchen where Ivo cooked for the school. Now the weather bell was ringing for an overnight frost. There were blankets in the shed, but the branches were too low to drape carelessly. “The bees ruined the early blossoms,” Mara said, repeating the explanation she had heard all week. “Perhaps covering these will keep them out.” Ivo looked at the darkening sky. They had a few hours, and no spare spring.

At the end of the first row, Mara bent over the damaged branch they had kept for comparison. The unopened buds were black inside; even flowers under a fine mesh bag had the same injury. A dated frost marker beside the branch matched the night of the damage. Bees had never entered that bag. “Cold,” she said at last. “We have been blaming the thing that brings us fruit.” The discovery did not mend a single blossom. It did change what she meant to shut out tonight.

Ivo brought out his father's field notebook, its swollen leather cover held shut with string. On the orchard bench, Mara opened a page showing cloth raised clear of the flowers and edges anchored against the ground. The words and drawing belonged together: a pocket of still air, not a wet blanket pressing on petals. But the bell kept pulling her eyes toward the road, where she expected a late delivery. She caught only half the method, remembering the cloth more clearly than its spacing and anchors.

Mara tried the blanket on the comparison branch. It sagged against the flowers, and a loose edge lifted every time the wind crossed the bench. The little cup beneath it cooled as quickly as its uncovered twin. She took the blanket off before its damp weight bent the twig. “That isn't a shelter yet,” she admitted. The failed trial spared the orchard a false promise, but it had consumed the last margin of light. They still had cloth, and still lacked a working way to use it.

Ivo held out his long fork, the one with his initials burned into the handle. It could lift cloth above the branches without tearing it. “Borrow it until morning,” he said. Mara took it carefully; the tool was still his, even while her hands controlled it. Then he shouldered the empty school baskets. The kitchen roof had sprung a leak, and he had to go. Mara would have to lift the cloth and fetch every stone herself. The loan helped, but it could not give her another pair of hands.

Mara carried the fork between the rows, then stopped. The bench trial had given her no working shelter to repeat. Raising a sheet for a moment did not keep it raised through a windy night. She folded the blankets beside the gate instead of loading the blossoms with wet cloth. Ivo's tool lay across the folded heap, useful and insufficient. Six white ribbons moved freely above six exposed rows as the first frost silvered the latch.

At sunrise Mara returned to the ribbons. Every exposed row held black-centered flowers. No living blossoms remained among the six marked rows. She counted carefully: no rows had surviving blossoms. There was no quarrel left to have with the bees. Cold had done this, and the night's work had determined how much of it the orchard could withstand.

Mara wiped the fork's handle and returned it to Ivo. His initials emerged from beneath the soil. She gathered all six white ribbons and laid them beside the shut notebook. Beyond the bench, a bee landed on an unmarked weed in bloom. Mara left it undisturbed.
