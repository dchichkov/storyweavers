The White Ribbons

Mara had tied white ribbons to the six orchard rows that morning. Each ribbon marked fruit promised to a different kitchen, including the kitchen where Ivo cooked for the school. Now the weather bell was ringing for an overnight frost. There were blankets in the shed, but the branches were too low to drape carelessly. “The bees ruined the early blossoms,” Mara said, repeating the explanation she had heard all week. “Perhaps covering these will keep them out.” Ivo looked at the darkening sky. They had a few hours, and no spare spring.

At the end of the first row, Mara bent over the damaged branch they had kept for comparison. The unopened buds were black inside; even flowers under a fine mesh bag had the same injury. A dated frost marker beside the branch matched the night of the damage. Bees had never entered that bag. “Cold,” she said at last. “We have been blaming the thing that brings us fruit.” The discovery did not mend a single blossom. It did change what she meant to shut out tonight.

Ivo brought out his father's field notebook, its swollen leather cover held shut with string. On the orchard bench, Mara opened a page showing cloth raised clear of the flowers and edges anchored against the ground. The words and drawing belonged together: a pocket of still air, not a wet blanket pressing on petals. She traced the drawing with a dirty finger, then said both instructions aloud. Ivo waited while she checked the page once more. This time she had attended to the whole method.

Mara made a shelter over the comparison branch: two short sticks, a roof of cloth, and stones closing the edges. She slid her hand underneath to check that no petal touched the fabric. A little cup of warm water remained warmer under that pocket than its twin on the bench. The small trial held its warmth. “That is something we can build,” she said. For the first time that evening, the blankets looked like a method instead of a heap of hopeful laundry.

Ivo held out his long fork, the one with his initials burned into the handle. It could lift cloth above the branches without tearing it. “Borrow it until morning,” he said. Mara took it carefully; the tool was still his, even while her hands controlled it. Then he shouldered the empty school baskets. The kitchen roof had sprung a leak, and he had to go. Mara would have to lift the cloth and fetch every stone herself. The loan helped, but it could not give her another pair of hands.

Alone, Mara finished two rows before the soil began to whiten. Every sheet meant a journey for supports, another for stones, and a careful lift with Ivo's fork. She checked those two shelters instead of beginning a third she could not finish. Four white ribbons still fluttered over exposed branches. She put down the last stone with shaking fingers. Saving only part of the promised fruit hurt, but leaving an unfinished roof would have made that promise smaller still.

At sunrise Mara returned to the ribbons. Beneath the two finished roofs the flowers remained firm; on the other four rows their centers had blackened. She counted carefully: two rows had surviving blossoms. There was no quarrel left to have with the bees. Cold had done this, and the night's work had determined how much of it the orchard could withstand.

Mara wiped the fork's handle and returned it to Ivo. His initials emerged from beneath the soil. She retied two ribbons beneath the surviving flowers and folded the other four into her coat. The two bright marks stayed visible from the gate.
